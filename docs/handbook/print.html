Foundations · Chapter 01

Work like an inventor

Start hereNo prior handbook chapters required.

A sensor needs to stay pointed at a moving target. You can imagine a printed bracket, a motorized mount, a camera that follows the target, or a robot that carries the sensor. All four are plausible projects. They are very different first experiments.

Start by asking what you need to learn next. If the sensor's location is still uncertain, a cardboard mount can answer more than a beautifully modeled enclosure. If the location is known but the bracket droops, a load and deflection test is useful. If the bracket is stable but the readings jump, another mechanical redesign may be irrelevant. Inventing becomes less mysterious when each build is a way to resolve a particular uncertainty.

This chapter assumes no specialist tools. By the end, you should be able to turn a broad idea into a small test, distinguish a requirement from a solution, and explain what evidence would justify the next version. You will need paper, a ruler, tape, and an ordinary object you would like to position or hold. A cardboard substitute is enough.

Begin with the job, then define the boundary

“The device should be better” gives you no way to decide when to stop. “The sensor should return to the same position after I remove and reinstall it” is more useful, but still needs a context. How close is close enough? Who installs it? With which tools? In what environment?

Write a sentence with a person, an action and a situation. For example: “At my indoor workbench, I want to remove a small sensor to change its cable and reinstall it without re-aiming it from scratch.” You have described a job. A dovetail, a magnet and two locating pins are possible solutions. Keeping these separate makes it easier to change the mechanism without forgetting why it exists.

The system boundary separates what you are designing from what you accept as given. For the first bracket, the bench, sensor and cable may be outside the boundary. Their mounting dimensions and loads still cross it. Draw a box around your design and arrows for every crossing: forces, electrical power, data, heat and human actions. An ordinary cable can cross three boundaries at once: it carries signals, consumes connector space and pulls on the mount.

This is also where you decide what this version will not attempt. A bench fixture need not survive rain unless outdoor use is part of its actual job. It does need enough cable access to be assembled. A useful scope boundary removes irrelevant work while retaining conditions that can make the intended use fail.

Requirements describe observations

A requirement is a statement you can check. It is not automatically a scientifically justified threshold. Your first numbers may be provisional; mark them as such and explain how you chose them.

Vague wishTestable first requirementWhy it matters
Easy to removeA user can remove and replace the sensor using one specified toolTests the service sequence rather than the appearance
AccurateAfter five reinstallations, a marked point remains within a provisional 1 mm target regionMakes repositioning visible; the threshold still needs application evidence
StrongHolds a stated test object in a defined orientation without slipping during a supervised bench checkIdentifies load, pose and failure; does not establish an unrestricted load rating
CompactFits inside a measured envelope while leaving the connector removableIncludes the space used during maintenance

A constraint is a boundary you must respect: a mounting-hole position, available supply voltage, fabrication process or budget. An objective is something you would like to improve within those constraints: mass, cost, noise or assembly time. If you combine every objective into “best,” the tradeoffs disappear from the conversation but remain in the object.

Stanford's Design Thinking Bootleg offers practical methods for understanding a situation, generating options and testing them. Use one method to investigate a real decision. Collecting an entire vocabulary of design methods is less useful than observing one person struggle with an actual connector.

Three representations of the same idea

Keep three views beside one another:

  1. A description: what the device should accomplish and for whom.
  2. A model: a sketch, equation, circuit or program that predicts something about it.
  3. An experiment: an action that can reveal whether the prediction is useful.

Suppose a thin plastic strip supports the sensor. Your description says the sensor must keep its aim. Your model says a longer unsupported span bends more. Your experiment compares two spans under the same small load. These are connected, but they are not interchangeable. A CAD image is a geometric model; it is not evidence that a particular printed material will hold its position for a week.

Every model leaves things out. A simple beam equation may ignore a flexible clamp. A circuit calculation may treat the supply as an ideal voltage source. A robot simulation may assume perfect friction. Write down the omission when it could change your decision. You do not need a complete model of the universe to make a bracket; you need a model whose limits you can recognize.

Choose the next experiment by information gained

Imagine you have four hours available. Printing a full enclosure takes three, and testing it takes another. A paper connector template takes ten minutes and can reveal that the cable cannot bend into the available space. When that uncertainty is unresolved, the template has a better learning return.

A simple planning table helps. Use ordinal scores as a conversation aid, not a mathematically precise forecast.

You can quantify part of this choice without pretending to know failure probabilities. If a fit coupon uses 8 g of filament and a full part uses 160 g, the full part consumes the material of 20 coupons. That does not prove the coupon predicts every behavior of the full part. It explains why local fit is worth checking locally while reserving full-size tests for span, warping and structural behavior that depend on scale.

Predict: would a half-size bracket establish the strength of the full-size bracket? Check: no. Geometry, load, layer arrangement and failure mechanisms do not all scale in the same way. A small specimen can answer a defined local question; it cannot silently stand in for every property of the final design.

A notebook that supports decisions

Give every experiment an identifier. Record the question, the design revision, the test setup, the raw result and the conclusion separately. “Hole too small” loses useful information. “Coupon F03, modeled 8.1 mm, PLA, mouth upward, sleeve enters by hand after loose debris removal” preserves the conditions of the observation.

Keep the raw readings even when they look inconvenient. A transcription error can be corrected with a note; silently replacing a surprising value makes it impossible to distinguish a measurement problem from a real event. Photographs should include a scale or identifiable reference when size matters. A file named final-final-real.stl is a warning that your revision system has become interpretive art.

A practical directory can be small:

Keep the editable source and the manufactured output together by revision. Changing a Fusion parameter does not update an STL already sent to a printer. The software and data chapter extends this habit to code, configuration and test inputs.

Bench lab: make a reversible positioning fixture

Choose an object light enough that a fall onto the table will not damage it; use a cardboard dummy if uncertain. Keep it away from people, fragile equipment and table edges. The lab investigates repeatable placement, not load capacity.

  1. Trace the object's outline on paper. Mark the location of a cable or access door if it has one.
  2. Make a crude holder with folded card and tape. Add two visible reference marks, one on the holder and one on the object.
  3. Write a test before performing it: remove the object, replace it, and measure the reference-mark displacement five times. Specify the ruler direction and what counts as the same location.
  4. Perform the sequence. Record every reading and note whether the object rocks, the card flexes or the tape slides.
  5. Change one feature: add a locating stop, a wider base or a second contact. Repeat the same sequence.
  6. Compare the ranges and the assembly experience. Decide which uncertainty to investigate next.

Expected observation: a physical stop often makes placement less dependent on visual alignment, but a flexible stop may move with the object. A smaller spread after the change supports an improvement under this test. It does not establish performance in other orientations or after repeated wear.

If the readings are indistinguishable: your ruler or marking method may be too coarse to see the difference, or both concepts may already be adequate for the chosen requirement. Improve the measurement or stop optimizing that property. A test that cannot discriminate between designs should not be turned into a confident ranking.

If the holder shifts on the table: first separate movement of the holder from movement within the holder. Otherwise you may redesign a locating feature to compensate for an unrelated attachment problem.

Use a makerspace as a learning system

A shared shop offers more than expensive machines. It provides examples, experienced operators and feedback on your setup. Bring a drawing and a question: “How would you hold this stock while leaving these two faces accessible?” invites a more useful conversation than “Can this machine make my part?”

Learn the local authorization and cleanup rules before operating a machine. For the immediate lab, the relevant hazards are modest. As projects grow, identify the energy involved: a hot nozzle, rotating tool, stored spring, charged capacitor or moving actuator each changes the test setup. Choose a way to stop and de-energize the experiment before increasing that energy. Machine-specific instruction belongs beside the actual machine and its trained operator; reading a general chapter is not a substitute.

Leave with one decision, not a shopping spree

Fill in the project brief. Your first independent task is to name one uncertain interface and make the cheapest prototype that can resolve it. An interface is where two things meet: a bolt and hole, hand and handle, sensor and software, or message and receiver.

Before moving on, answer these questions in your notebook:

  • What would count as a failed experiment? A result you dislike can still be informative. A setup that cannot answer the stated question is the more fundamental failure.
  • Which part of the result is measured, and which part is inferred? Five stable placements are measured evidence. A prediction about a thousand placements requires an additional argument or test.
  • What will the next version change? Name one cause you intend to address and the observation that will tell you whether it helped.

The bracket is only the beginning. The same cycle applies when your object becomes a circuit, a program or a robot: define the job, expose the assumptions, run a discriminating experiment, and keep enough evidence to make the next decision intelligible.

Math and physics at the workbench

Builds on01 · Work like an inventor

A bracket is 80 mm long. Its payload has a mass of 0.25 kg. Those two measurements already let you make a useful prediction: extending the payload farther from its support increases the turning effect at the support. You do not need a full simulation to notice that consequence. You need a sketch, a clear definition of distance and a few quantities whose units stay attached.

This chapter develops that small set of tools. You will convert units, rearrange a relationship, resolve a direction into components and distinguish force, energy and power. Bring the project brief from Chapter 1, a ruler, paper and a calculator with trigonometric functions. Algebra helps; the examples show the substitutions rather than assuming you remember every formula. Calculus is a later extension, not an entrance examination.

Units are part of the calculation

A number without a unit leaves the reader guessing. Write 80 mm, not just 80, in a drawing note, data table or program variable description. A calculation can use any consistent units, but a set built around kilograms, metres, seconds and amperes avoids many surprises in mechanics and electricity.

Conversion is multiplication by a ratio equal to one:

80 mm × (1 m / 1000 mm) = 0.080 m

The millimetres cancel. That cancellation is a useful diagnostic. If your moment calculation ends in newtons per metre when you expected newton-metres, the equation or substitution needs attention. Unit consistency cannot prove the model is appropriate, but inconsistency can disprove a calculation before you build anything.

Area and volume require extra care. A 10 mm × 10 mm square has an area of 100 mm². In metres, each side is 0.01 m, so its area is 0.0001 m². There are one million square millimetres per square metre, not one thousand. Similarly, 1 cm³ is 1000 mm³. This is why a plausible looking density calculation can be wrong by orders of magnitude.

Moment and energy share dimensions, but describe different things. Do not convert a shaft's torque into energy without also knowing its angular displacement. Names and physical interpretation matter alongside units.

One model, with its assumptions visible

For a stationary payload near Earth's surface, use F = mg, with g ≈ 9.81 m/s². A 0.25 kg payload has a downward weight of about 2.45 N. If that force acts 0.080 m horizontally from a support axis, its moment magnitude is:

M = Fd = 2.45 N × 0.080 m ≈ 0.196 N·m

This is a planar example: the arm and force lie in the page, and the support axis points perpendicular to the page. Here d is the perpendicular distance from the support point to the force's line of action. It is not always the length of the bracket. If the same 80 mm arm points vertically and gravity acts along it, the moment from that idealized force about its base is zero. The support still carries a force. If the arm points upward at 45° from horizontal, the perpendicular distance is 0.080 cos(45°) ≈ 0.0566 m, and the moment is about 0.139 N·m. In three dimensions, moment is a vector; its component along a chosen axis determines the turning effect about that axis.

These are static weight calculations. They omit the bracket's own weight, acceleration, cable tension, impacts, joint friction and assembly eccentricity. List those omissions before using the result to choose hardware. In the pivot project, the neighboring module's mass and centre of mass remain inputs to measure. The presence of two bearings does not turn this arithmetic into a validated load rating.

The dashed distance is perpendicular to the downward force. Rotating the arm changes that distance even when its length stays constant.

A relationship is also a way to ask what matters. M = mgd says doubling mass doubles moment, doubling perpendicular distance doubles moment, and doubling both multiplies moment by four. Before refining decimal places, check whether you have chosen the right mass and distance.

Solve for the quantity you need

Suppose an early experiment is limited to a hypothetical moment of 0.10 N·m, chosen solely as a planning example. With a 0.25 kg mass, what distance corresponds to that moment?

Start with M = mgd. Divide both sides by mg:

d = M / (mg) = 0.10 / (0.25 × 9.81) ≈ 0.0408 m = 40.8 mm

This is an algebraic result, not permission to load a particular printed part. You must establish a suitable test limit separately. The distinction matters: equations describe relationships; allowable values come from evidence, requirements and applicable practice.

Estimation gives you a second check. Round gravity to 10 N/kg and distance to 0.04 m. Then 0.25 × 10 × 0.04 = 0.10 N·m. Agreement at that scale makes a misplaced decimal less likely. If the detailed answer were 40.8 metres, the rough calculation would expose it.

Directions become components

A 50 mm line at 30° counterclockwise from a horizontal positive x-axis has components:

x = 50 cos(30°) ≈ 43.3 mm

y = 50 sin(30°) = 25.0 mm

Those two distances reconstruct the endpoint. Their squared sum is the squared length: 43.3² + 25.0² ≈ 50². The small discrepancy comes from rounding. Change the angle to −30°, and x stays positive while y becomes negative. The sign records direction rather than an impossible negative physical length.

This is the beginning of vectors. A vector has magnitude and direction; its component numbers depend on a coordinate frame. Label the origin and positive axes on your sketch. Later, a robot's camera and arm will each have their own frame, and transforming between them will be a central task. For now, use a single frame consistently.

Watch the calculator's angle mode. Many programming libraries accept radians. A half-turn is π radians or 180°, so 30° × π/180 ≈ 0.524 rad. Passing 30 to a function expecting radians asks a different question. State the unit in variable names such as angle_deg and angle_rad when you write the software lab.

Change, accumulation and conservation

A sensor that moves 20 mm in 0.5 s has an average speed of 40 mm/s. That does not establish its speed at every instant. A plot of position against time makes the distinction visible: slope describes rate of change. If the slope changes, the speed changes; a changing velocity implies acceleration.

The reverse idea is accumulation. If a load draws a steady 2 W for 10 minutes, it consumes 2 × 600 = 1200 J, or about 0.333 Wh. Doubling operating time doubles that energy when power stays constant. If power varies, split the time into small intervals, multiply each interval's power by its duration and add the contributions. Differentiation formalizes local rate; integration formalizes accumulation. Those concepts will recur in control and battery budgeting.

Energy accounting prevents another class of impossible design. Raising 0.25 kg by 0.10 m increases gravitational potential energy by about mgh = 0.245 J. Completing that lift in 0.50 s requires an average mechanical output of at least 0.49 W for the lift alone. Electrical input must also supply losses and any other work. A motor's shaft torque specification alone does not tell you whether it can perform the motion at the required speed.

Heat belongs in the same accounting. A device continuously losing 3 W as heat puts 180 J into its surroundings each minute. Whether it overheats depends on the path from the source to the environment, including conduction, convection and radiation. An enclosure can obstruct that path. A temperature reading at the outside of a case is not automatically the hottest internal component's temperature. Leave thermal limits as a measured design question rather than assuming low voltage means no heating.

Scale changes more than appearance

Imagine uniformly scaling a solid part by a factor of two, keeping its material and shape proportions. Length doubles, surface area grows by four and volume grows by eight. At constant density, mass grows by eight. A larger part therefore has less surface area per unit volume. That can affect cooling and manufacturing as well as weight.

Real redesigns are often not uniform: you may increase an octagonal housing's radius while keeping strut thickness fixed. In that case, the simple cube law does not predict its mass. Count what actually changed. Likewise, removing material from the middle of a beam and removing it near an outer surface do not generally produce equal stiffness changes. Mechanics develops why placement matters.

Graphs help reveal such relationships. Put the controlled variable on the horizontal axis, the response on the vertical axis and units on both. A straight line through the origin suggests direct proportionality over the tested range. A curved trend is information, not a plotting mistake. Do not extend a fitted line far outside the measurements merely because the software can draw it.

Paper lab: predict a position before measuring it

Use paper, a ruler, a pencil and a protractor if available. No powered mechanism or hanging load is needed.

  1. Draw an origin and horizontal x-axis. Mark a 60 mm line at 0°, 30° and 60°, using separate sketches if they overlap.
  2. Before measuring the endpoints, calculate each expected x and y component. Record your calculator mode.
  3. Measure horizontal and vertical distances from each endpoint to the origin. Record to a precision your ruler can support; do not invent hundredths of a millimetre.
  4. Compare prediction and drawing. At 30°, expect approximately (52.0, 30.0) mm; at 60°, expect (30.0, 52.0) mm.
  5. Draw a downward force arrow through each endpoint. Measure the horizontal distance to that line and predict which orientation produces the largest gravity moment.

The 0° arm should have the largest horizontal distance. Disagreement may come from a protractor referenced to the wrong axis, exchanged sine and cosine, a nonhorizontal page reference or an angle-mode error. Diagnose the geometry before changing the equations.

These are expected observations, not measurements taken from your drawing. Keep your actual values. This simple exercise joins an abstract coordinate pair to a physical position, which is the same translation you will later make between CAD, a fixture and a robot frame.

Check your understanding

A 0.5 kg mass sits twice as far from an axis as a 0.25 kg mass. How do their moments compare under the same perpendicular geometry? The larger mass produces four times the moment: two times the force and two times the distance.

Does 0.196000 N·m describe the example better than 0.196 N·m? Extra digits in the calculator do not create better knowledge of mass, position or model assumptions. Retain enough digits during calculation, then report a precision appropriate to the inputs and purpose.

Can a static calculation establish impact strength? No. Impact involves motion, deformation, time and often a different load path. It needs a suitable model and experiment.

What mathematical topic should you learn next? Choose from the obstacle in your project: algebra for rearranging relationships, trigonometry for geometry, statistics for variable measurements, calculus for changing systems, or linear algebra for coordinates and coupled equations.

For independent practice, make a small table of payload mass, arm angle and predicted static moment for your own proposed sensor mount. Mark every assumed value. Use the workbench tools to check the arithmetic, then explain one result without the tool.

For deeper study, OpenStax College Physics 2e is an algebra-based reference; Precalculus 2e supplies the algebra and trigonometry bridge. 3Blue1Brown's vector lesson helps connect arrows and components. When rates and accumulation become a recurring need, MIT's Single Variable Calculus provides archived videos and problems for independent study. Start with a specific question and work a problem after watching.

Measure, experiment and debug

Builds on01 · Work like an inventor02 · Math and physics at the workbench

Your printed shaft hole looks round and your caliper displays 8.12 mm. The shaft still will not enter. Which claim failed: the drawing, the manufacturing process, the measurement or the assumption that a single diameter describes the fit? You cannot answer by adding more decimal places. You need to define what you are measuring and how the assembly uses that feature.

In this chapter you will distinguish resolution, repeatability and bias; make a small experimental comparison; and record enough context for another person to interpret the result. Use units and arithmetic from Chapter 2. Bring a ruler or caliper if you have one, a rigid object with accessible faces and a notebook. A ruler-based placement experiment works without buying a precision instrument.

Define the question before choosing the instrument

“Measure the hole” can mean diameter at its entrance, diameter farther down, roundness, taper or whether a particular shaft slides through. These are related but different questions. A chamfer can make the entrance larger than the cylindrical region that controls fit. Caliper jaws can contact two high points and miss a local restriction. A functional gauge or the intended mating part may answer the assembly question more directly, provided you do not force it into the part.

Write a measurement statement such as: “Measure the outside width between these two marked faces, at the centre of the part, after it has cooled to room conditions.” Name the instrument, reference surfaces, contact method and temperature when relevant. Photograph or sketch the location. This definition makes later comparisons meaningful.

Resolution is the smallest displayed or distinguishable increment. Accuracy concerns closeness to a reference value. Repeatability concerns agreement when the measurement is repeated under specified conditions. A display reading to 0.01 mm does not establish ±0.01 mm accuracy. A zero check is useful, but does not by itself calibrate the instrument over its whole range.

The range is an easy description of the observed spread, but depends strongly on how many samples you took. Standard deviation summarizes deviations from the mean; it is not the largest possible error.

If these readings are independent observations under stable conditions, the estimated standard uncertainty of their mean from this repeatability contribution is s/√n ≈ 0.0071 mm. That reduction applies to the estimate of the mean. It does not say each individual part or future reading varies by only 0.0071 mm. It also says nothing about an instrument offset shared by all five readings.

Suppose a suitable reference check reveals a +0.08 mm offset under the relevant conditions. Subtracting the estimated offset changes the corrected mean to 19.95 mm. The correction itself has uncertainty. Taking fifty readings without checking the reference could produce a very consistent answer to the wrong value.

NIST distinguishes uncertainty evaluated through statistical analysis of observations, called Type A, from uncertainty evaluated using other information, called Type B. These are methods of evaluation, not alternative names for random and systematic error. Its Technical Note 1297 is a useful deeper reference once you need a defensible uncertainty budget.

A useful uncertainty budget starts with causes

For a fit experiment, list plausible influences before calculating a single combined number:

  • Instrument resolution and its applicable specification or calibration information.
  • Jaw placement, tilt and contact force, especially on a flexible printed wall.
  • Surface texture, seam, burrs, taper and the location being measured.
  • Temperature, moisture and time after manufacturing where they affect the material.
  • Variation between repeated readings, between features and between separately manufactured parts.

Those last three variations are not interchangeable. Measuring one part ten times explores measurement repeatability. Measuring ten parts once explores a mixture of part variation and measurement variation. Neither design alone completely separates the two.

A formal combined standard uncertainty commonly uses a measurement model and combines contributions with appropriate sensitivities and correlations. A beginner can still make a disciplined decision without claiming a formal confidence interval. State the observed range, the instrument limitations and the unmeasured influences. If a decision depends on a 0.02 mm difference and your method cannot reliably distinguish it, improve the method or redesign the interface to be less sensitive.

Avoid reporting ± without saying what it means. “Mean 20.03 mm; five readings ranged from 20.01 to 20.05 mm” is clearer than “20.03 ±0.02 mm” with no definition. For a critical acceptance decision, uncertainty and the acceptance rule need explicit treatment; a displayed number barely inside a limit is weak evidence.

Design a comparison that can answer its question

Imagine testing whether a cardboard stop improves the repeatability of placing a sensor mockup. The response is the measured position of the sensor's reference edge. The deliberate change is presence or absence of the stop. Keep the object, surface, reference line, measurement method and operator procedure constant enough to interpret the comparison.

Write predictions before taking data: “The stop will reduce the spread of placement positions” is different from “The stop will move the average position toward 40 mm.” A device can become more repeatable while becoming consistently wrong. Measure both centre and spread.

If all free trials occur first, practice or fatigue may confound the comparison. Alternate or randomize the order where practical, and record it. Randomization does not remove every influence; it reduces the chance that a time-related influence always coincides with one condition. Repeating a whole experiment on another day tests something different from repeatedly reading the same placement.

One variable at a time has limits

Changing one factor while keeping others fixed is a good first diagnostic. It lets you connect a changed response to a deliberate intervention more easily. But factors can interact. A print temperature that improves bonding in one orientation may not provide the same practical benefit in another geometry. A flexible sensor mount may behave differently only when a cable is attached.

A small two-factor comparison can reveal that interaction. Test both configurations with and without the cable, using the same loading and measurement procedure. Compare the cable's effect within each configuration. If the effect changes substantially, the factors interact over the tested conditions. Do not average away the difference and declare one setting universally best.

More advanced design of experiments formalizes factor selection, replication, randomization and response models. The NIST/SEMATECH handbook's experimental-design section is a deeper route. At the bench, your first responsibility is simpler: preserve what changed, what stayed fixed and what was actually observed.

Debug by separating possible causes

A failure such as “the sensor reading jumps” is a symptom, not a diagnosis. Draw the path: physical stimulus → sensor → wiring → conversion → software → display. Then find a measurement that separates explanations.

If a known constant input produces stable raw readings but an unstable displayed value, the later software path deserves attention. If touching the cable changes raw readings, inspect the physical connection and electrical reference. If the problem appears only when a motor starts, power, grounding, electromagnetic coupling and mechanical motion become competing hypotheses. Several may contribute at once.

Use a short hypothesis table. For each explanation, write what observation would support it, what would contradict it and the cheapest safe test. Change one relevant thing, retain the previous working state and record the outcome. Randomly replacing five components can remove the symptom while leaving you unable to explain or reproduce the repair.

Negative evidence is useful only when the test could have detected the effect. “I did not see deflection” means little if the ruler could not resolve it or the camera moved with the fixture. Establish the measurement's reference and sensitivity.

Bench lab: separate placement from reading

Download the experiment log. Use a ruler, a small rigid block or folded card sensor mockup, tape and a flat table. Use a removable card stop; no sharp tool or powered equipment is necessary.

  1. Mark a reference line and a target position suitable for your ruler, perhaps 40 mm. Define which edge of the mockup is measured.
  2. Place the mockup once and read its position five times without moving it. Look away between readings. Record every result. This mainly probes your reading method, though the object may drift.
  3. Remove and replace it five times, measuring once after each placement. This adds placement variation.
  4. Add the stop and repeat five replacements. Alternate conditions if the stop can be removed reproducibly; otherwise note the order as a limitation.
  5. Calculate the mean and range for each set. Describe any shift in the mean separately from the change in spread.
  6. Ask another person to repeat the method using only your written instructions, or repeat it later without referring to your earlier values.

Expected outcome: repeated placements may vary more than repeated readings of an unmoved object, and a stable stop may reduce placement variation. Your apparatus may behave differently. If all readings are identical, check whether the ruler is simply too coarse to reveal the differences. If the stop performs worse, inspect tape movement, flexible edges and inconsistent contact pressure. Keep the result; the experiment is allowed to reject your prediction.

Check your understanding

Will averaging remove a constant zero offset? No. It can reduce an independent repeatability contribution to uncertainty in the mean; a shared offset remains until addressed through a suitable correction or method.

Do twenty measurements of one printed hole establish production consistency? No. They mainly describe repeated measurement of that feature. You need separately manufactured parts to study part-to-part variation.

The improved fixture is more repeatable but misses the target. Is it a failure? It has solved one problem and exposed another. Decide whether the offset can be corrected without losing repeatability, then test the revised configuration.

Must every experiment use a formal statistical test? No. Early exploratory work often needs transparent raw data and a large, reproducible practical effect. A formal test cannot rescue a poorly defined measurement or confounded comparison.

For independent practice, choose one disputed fit, sensor behavior or placement in your project. Write two competing explanations and design a measurement that distinguishes them. Use a provisional acceptance rule before seeing the result. In Chapter 4, you will connect such evidence to a user's actual task, where a technically measurable improvement may still be the wrong thing to optimize.

Design for people and purpose

A sensor bracket can hold a sensor securely and still be unpleasant to use. Perhaps the cable cannot be unplugged, the adjustment screw needs three hands, or reinstalling the sensor changes its aim. Mechanical performance is one part of the product. The complete job includes the person who sets it up, the surrounding equipment and the moments when something needs repair.

You will turn an observed task into requirements, compare distinct concepts and run a small usability experiment. Bring the project brief from Chapter 1 and the measurement habits from Chapter 3. The prototype can be cardboard. Its purpose is to reveal an interaction before you invest in detailed CAD.

Observe a task before asking for a feature

“Would you like an adjustable bracket?” encourages agreement with your idea. “Show me how you position this sensor now” exposes the current process. Watch where the person pauses, changes grip, searches for a tool or repeats an alignment. Ask what happened during a specific recent attempt. Separate what you saw from your interpretation.

For example, you might observe that someone disconnects a cable before removing a sensor. Your first interpretation is that the cable is in the way. A follow-up may reveal that disconnecting it prevents the software from recording unwanted data. A larger cable opening would not solve that problem. The observation is real; the cause still needs investigation.

Keep three columns: observation, possible need and question to test. “The user held the sensor with one hand while tightening two screws” is an observation. “The user needs temporary support during adjustment” is a candidate need. “Would a locating ledge allow adjustment without holding the sensor?” is a testable question. This chain makes the reasoning available for revision.

The Stanford d.school Bootleg offers methods for observing, defining, generating ideas, prototyping and testing. Use a method to answer a real project question; the names of the stages are not evidence that a concept works.

Requirements describe outcomes and constraints

A useful requirement names a condition and a way to check it. “Easy to assemble” is a direction. “A new user can install the dummy sensor using the supplied instructions and one hex key, without dropping a loose nut” is closer to an observable test. You still need to decide how many users and attempts provide enough confidence for your purpose.

Separate essential constraints from preferences. A maximum envelope may be essential because the assembly must clear another part. A particular color may be a preference. A mounting-hole pattern may be an interface constraint inherited from a purchased sensor. If a requirement fixes a solution too early, record why that restriction exists.

Allow maintenanceReplace the sensor without dismantling the main supportAssembly sequence and tool-access trial

Do not copy a threshold from a tutorial and assume it matches your job. Derive it from the consequence of error. If a sensor's field of view is broad, a millimetre of mounting variation may be irrelevant. If two gears must maintain centre distance, the same variation can prevent operation. Requirements should connect to behavior.

Generate concepts that differ in principle

Three sketches with different corner radii are usually one concept. To explore alternatives, change how the task is achieved. A sensor could rest against locating stops and use one retaining screw; slide into a rail and latch; or sit in an adjustable cradle with a separate lock. Each arrangement makes different promises about repeatability, access and adjustment.

Break the task into functions: locate, retain, adjust, connect and release. Sketch alternative ways to perform each function. Then combine compatible choices. This prevents the first attractive silhouette from dictating every subsequent decision.

A functional decomposition is not an instruction to make every piece modular. The pivot's integrated triangulated housing may combine stiffness, access and appearance in one printed part. The question is whether combining functions helps the particular requirements. Separate components introduce fasteners and tolerances; integrated components can complicate printing and repair. Count both effects.

A weighted comparison is a conversation with your assumptions

Suppose three concepts pass the essential envelope and access constraints. You score each from 1 to 5, where 5 is better, on repeatability, ease of assembly and ease of fabrication. Assign weights of 0.5, 0.3 and 0.2 respectively:

ConceptRepeatabilityAssemblyFabricationWeighted score
Locating stops + screw4353.9
Slide rail + latch4523.9

The first score is 0.5×4 + 0.3×3 + 0.2×5 = 3.9. The tie is useful. It tells you the table does not resolve the choice under these assumptions. The rail's assembly advantage and fabrication disadvantage offset each other. A small rail coupon or a first-use mockup can supply evidence where the scores were guesses.

Changing weights changes the answer. If assembly becomes dominant, the rail may lead. If fabrication time dominates, the stops may lead. Do a sensitivity check before treating the highest score as an objective fact. Do not average an essential failure into a respectable total: a concept that blocks the connector is disqualified until that constraint is addressed.

Record whether each score came from observation, calculation, supplier information or intuition. The table's precision should not hide the uncertainty of its inputs.

Prototype the question, not the entire product

A cardboard model can test hand clearance and the order of assembly. It cannot establish the strength of a printed latch. A small printed coupon can test a sliding fit. It cannot establish the usability of a complete installation. Choose the prototype's fidelity according to the uncertainty you want to reduce.

For the bracket, three focused prototypes may be more useful than one polished assembly:

  1. A full-size paper or card envelope to test placement and connector access.
  2. A small mating-feature coupon to test manufacturing clearance.
  3. A simplified complete bracket to test installation and repeatability.

The prototypes answer different questions. Label each with its purpose and excluded claims. If you substitute a dummy sensor, match the dimensions that matter to the test and record which others are approximate. A dummy without the real cable may give a misleadingly spacious impression.

In the pivot case study, the interactive assembly can expose a hidden fastener or show where two parts meet. It is valuable for spatial understanding. It does not reproduce finger access, print texture, friction or the force required to assemble a real bearing. Use it to plan the next physical test.

Make action and feedback legible

A user must discover what can be done, perform the action and recognize its effect. A recessed screw that resembles a pivot pin may invite the wrong action. A knob that turns without a visible change may leave the user uncertain whether it is locked. Shape, labels, movement limits and feedback can reduce those ambiguities.

Physical arrangement can communicate a relationship: a control located beside the axis it adjusts is easier to associate with that axis. A captured nut can make assembly less dependent on dexterity. A keyed shape can prevent an unintended orientation, but should not require force to reveal that orientation is wrong. Test with the actual use conditions, including visibility, reach and any gloves or tools involved.

Color can reinforce information, but pair it with shape, position or text. In the pivot viewer, named parts complement colors. In a physical device, a tactile stop or orientation mark can remain useful when lighting is poor. Design for the range of people and circumstances relevant to the job rather than assuming your own hands and familiarity represent everyone.

Don Norman's The Design of Everyday Things is a deeper reading route for these human-centered questions. It complements the mechanical and electrical chapters; it does not supply their engineering validation.

Consider the whole life of a part

Assembly order changes geometry. A bolt may fit its hole yet have no path into position after neighboring parts are installed. A driver may reach the head but lack room to turn. Draw the insertion path, tool envelope and hand access. Try the sequence with ordinary tools before adding decorative details.

Repair raises similar questions. Which part is most likely to wear? Can it be replaced? Are fasteners accessible? Are cables strained when the enclosure opens? Reducing part count can simplify assembly, but bonding everything permanently can make a minor failure expensive. Prefer a deliberate tradeoff over a universal rule.

A quick cost model also broadens the decision. In an illustrative batch of ten brackets, assume each uses 25 g of material at an assumed $25/kg, 20 minutes of machine time and 6 minutes of hands-on finishing. Material is about $0.63 per part. That is not the total cost: failed prints, machine ownership, inserts, packaging and your time still exist. If a redesign saves one minute of material deposition but adds five minutes of manual cleanup, the material-only comparison misses the main consequence. Substitute your own prices and measured times; these are planning numbers, not a purchasing quote.

Lab: test an installation without coaching

Make two low-fidelity concepts for holding a harmless dummy sensor. Use card, tape and existing loose hardware only as nonloaded mockup elements. Do not rely on cardboard to restrain a powered or heavy device. Prepare a short instruction card and a clearly stated task, such as “Place the sensor against the reference and leave the connector accessible.”

Ask a willing adult to try one concept, then the other. Explain that you are testing the design, not the person. Observe their first action, requests for help, reversals and whether the task finishes. Avoid pointing out the intended sequence while the trial is underway. If something unsafe or damaging could happen, stop immediately; withholding help is not more important than the participant.

Afterward, ask what they expected each feature to do. Their explanation can reveal a mismatch you did not see. Record the exact observed difficulty, then change one feature and repeat. If you are working alone, put the prototype away and attempt it later from the instructions; recognize that this does not reproduce a new user's perspective.

Expected outcomes include discovering an ambiguous orientation, obstructed grip or missing instruction. A smooth trial is evidence for that participant and condition, not proof that all users will succeed. If neither concept produces a clear difference, choose a more demanding relevant task or retain the simpler adequate design.

Check your understanding

A user asks for a bigger knob. Should that become the requirement? First identify the underlying difficulty: grip force, reach, visibility or insufficient torque may each suggest a different solution. Preserve the observation and test the need.

The highest-scoring concept violates an essential access constraint. Can its other strengths compensate? No. Rework or reject it before comparing preferences.

What does a successful cardboard trial establish? Evidence about the represented geometry and interaction under that trial. It does not establish printed strength, thermal behavior or long-term wear.

Your independent task is to revise one requirement after observing a real task, then build a prototype aimed only at the most consequential uncertainty. Keep the old requirement and explain the change. Its materials are a study resource; you supply the project, collaborators and feedback.

CAD that explains how a part should change

A useful CAD model records decisions. If a sensor moves, its mounting holes should follow it. If a bracket gets longer, a purchased bearing should keep its actual diameter. A model that looks correct once but breaks when edited has captured a picture more successfully than an engineering idea.

This chapter teaches you to build a small parametric mounting plate, change it deliberately, and prepare it for someone else to manufacture. You need basic millimeter arithmetic and the measurement habits from the earlier chapters. Fusion is the example environment; the relationships also apply to other parametric solid modelers. A paper construction is provided if you cannot use CAD today.

Begin with interfaces, then choose the shape

Imagine a lightweight bench sensor with two mounting holes 24 mm apart. Its cable must leave one end without rubbing. The bracket mounts to a tabletop fixture and must fit within a 60 mm-wide space. These are three different kinds of information: a hardware interface, an access requirement, and an envelope constraint.

Do not turn every number into a freely adjustable design choice. Record which dimensions come from hardware, which are measured estimates, and which you selected. Changing a measured hole spacing to make the drawing prettier would break the interface. Changing a chosen border width might be harmless.

For our first plate, use the following exercise dimensions, not a claim about a particular sensor:

ParameterValueMeaning
plate_length50 mmChosen envelope along X
plate_width30 mmChosen envelope along Y
plate_thickness4 mmInitial prototype choice
hole_pitch24 mmSimulated sensor interface
hole_diameter3.4 mmInitial clearance-hole candidate
edge_chamfer0.5 mmInitial edge-break candidate

The hole diameter is not a universal recommendation for printed M3 joints. The actual screw, print process, and desired fit determine the final value. Its purpose here is to make the clearance an explicit decision that can later be tested with a coupon.

Choose a reference system before drawing. Put the plate center at the sketch origin, with its long direction along X. Locate the hole centers at X = −12 mm and +12 mm, both at Y = 0. Now the part is symmetrical by construction, and increasing its length adds equal material at both ends.

This reference is a datum choice: a stable basis for locating features. On a finished part, a datum may be an actual mounting face, bore axis, or edge. A digital origin is useful only when its relationship to those physical references makes sense.

The origin and symmetry keep the interface centered when the surrounding plate changes.

Constraints remove unintended freedom

An unconstrained circle in a plane has three adjustable quantities: its center’s X position, its center’s Y position, and its radius. A diameter dimension controls only the last one. The circle can still wander unless its center is located.

Geometric constraints express relationships: two lines remain perpendicular, two circles remain concentric, two lengths remain equal. Dimensions supply sizes or distances. Prefer a relationship when the relationship is the requirement. Two mounting holes that must share a diameter should be equal, rather than separately assigned the same number and left free to diverge later.

For the plate, a center rectangle establishes symmetry about the origin. Length and width finish its size. The two holes share a horizontal construction line through the origin, sit symmetrically about the vertical centerline, and have a center-to-center dimension of hole_pitch. Give one hole a diameter and make the second equal.

A fully constrained sketch has no unintended freedom left. It does not prove that your dimensions are correct. You can fully constrain the wrong hole pitch. Autodesk’s short design-intent module connects these ideas to parameter-driven parts and assemblies; its sketch-constraints tutorial demonstrates the tools.

Avoid using Fix merely to stop a sketch moving. Fix can freeze an accidental position without explaining it. Likewise, adding redundant dimensions can overconstrain a sketch: if equality already links two circles, independently controlling both diameters may impose the same requirement twice. Ask which fact each constraint expresses.

Build a short, readable feature history

Create a named component before creating the plate. In an assembly, a component represents a meaningful part with its own origin and motion relationships. A body is a connected solid within a component. Two solids intended to move independently usually deserve separate components; a temporary construction body does not automatically need one.

Create the plate outline sketch on an origin plane, then extrude it by plate_thickness. Name the sketch Plate outline and the feature Plate thickness. Create a second sketch for the holes and cut them through the plate. Through All communicates the intention better than a cut depth of 4 mm: if the plate becomes 6 mm thick, the holes should still pass through.

For this simple part, an origin-plane hole sketch and a through cut can be especially stable. Inspect the cut arrow: Through All still has a direction, and the cut must extend from the sketch through the body. Sketching on a generated face is convenient, but the reference may become fragile if earlier features split or replace that face. This is not a ban on face sketches. It is a reason to prefer durable references for important interfaces.

Add small edge treatments after the primary shape and holes exist. A chamfer replaces an edge with a flat bevel; a fillet replaces it with a rounded transition. They are functional features, not a final “make it engineered” command. A chamfer may guide assembly or relieve a bed-contact edge. A fillet can spread stress at a rib root, while also affecting tool access or printing overhangs.

Apply separate features for separate purposes. Name a hole-entry chamfer differently from an external cosmetic edge break. If one must change, the other should not be accidentally swept along. Avoid selecting every edge in the model just because the preview looks attractive.

Nominal geometry, tolerance, and resolution

A CAD hole of 3.4 mm is nominal geometry. A manufacturing tolerance describes the acceptable departure from a specified value. Printer layer height, mesh density, and a caliper’s display increment are forms of resolution; none establishes the accuracy of the finished hole.

Suppose an imaginary drawing allows a hole from 3.30 to 3.50 mm, and a mating pin can measure from 2.95 to 3.05 mm. The possible diametral clearance is:

minimum clearance = smallest hole − largest pin
                  = 3.30 − 3.05 = 0.25 mm
maximum clearance = largest hole − smallest pin
                  = 3.50 − 2.95 = 0.55 mm

For centered circular parts, the radial gap is half the diametral clearance. Calling 0.25 mm a “gap on each side” would therefore double the intended allowance. This simple limit calculation is more useful than assuming every nominal dimension arrives exactly as modeled.

Position also matters. Two individually generous holes can still refuse to assemble if their spacing is wrong. A single round hole plus a slot can sometimes accommodate one direction of variation, but it changes how the part locates and carries load. Do not add slots automatically; decide which directions need precise location and which can float.

For prototypes, record what you actually need to function: both screws insert by hand, the sensor sits flat, the cable clears, and tightening does not distort the plate. Later, translate critical requirements into a drawing and inspection method. Avoid attaching tight tolerances to every surface without a functional reason.

Lab: make one model survive three changes

Allow 45–75 minutes. Use Fusion, paper for notes, and optionally a ruler. No printer is needed.

  1. Create a new design in millimeters and name the component Sensor plate. Enter the six parameters from the table.
  2. Sketch the centered rectangle and constrain its size. Try dragging a corner before and after adding dimensions. Observe which freedoms disappear.
  3. Extrude the plate, create the symmetric hole layout, and cut through it. Name the features as you build.
  4. Add the selected external chamfers. Inspect a section or side view to verify that the bevel removes material rather than adding an unintended ledge.
  5. Save a baseline. Change plate_length from 50 to 60 mm. Predict the new end margin before examining the result.
  6. Restore 50 mm, then change hole_pitch from 24 to 30 mm. Finally, change thickness from 4 to 6 mm. Check that the holes remain through and the component remains centered.
  7. Restore the baseline and capture a top view, a side view, and the parameter table. Add a short note explaining why each parameter exists.

The expected center-to-end margin for the original holes is (50 − 24)/2 = 13 mm. At 60 mm plate length it becomes 18 mm. At 30 mm pitch in the original plate it becomes 10 mm. These are distances to hole centers; the remaining material beyond the hole edge is smaller by its radius.

If a hole stays behind during an edit, inspect whether it was dimensioned from an unrelated edge or fixed in place. If the cut stops inside the thicker plate, inspect its extent. If a chamfer fails, suppress it temporarily and check whether the underlying design still works. A downstream edge failure should not force you to redraw the entire part.

The paper alternative is to draw the origin, centerlines, rectangle, and holes at full scale. Repeat the three variants on tracing paper. Label the relationships that must survive. This teaches design intent even though paper cannot enforce the constraints for you.

These are expected outcomes of the exercise, not a report that a physical sensor plate has been manufactured or fitted.

Deliver a model someone can use

Keep the editable native design with its parameters and history. Exporting a mesh does not preserve that design logic. STEP is useful for exchanging solid geometry, while STL describes a triangulated surface and carries no reliable unit declaration in the format itself. A 3MF package can carry units and additional information, but a slicer project may also contain machine-specific settings. Tell the recipient what each file is for.

Before handing off, reopen the exported file and check the bounding dimensions. An incorrectly interpreted unit can create a beautifully detailed part at the wrong scale. Inspect curved holes for obvious faceting and confirm that each intended part is a closed solid. Finer mesh settings improve geometric approximation, not printer calibration.

Include a simple drawing showing critical mounting dimensions, material/process assumptions, and revision. An isometric image explains appearance; orthographic views explain dimensions. Avoid dimensioning everything from everything else. Locate related interfaces from shared references so a reviewer can see the chain of decisions.

In the triangulated pivot case study, the 150 mm across-flats envelope and 260 mm straight length are chosen geometry, while the 608 bearing interfaces and 8 mm shaft derive from hardware. The 25.1 mm bearing-center spacing is another deliberate geometric choice. Parameter edits must preserve those distinctions. The downloadable model is a teaching prototype, not evidence that the assembly has been physically tested.

Check your reasoning

Prediction: The plate width increases from 30 to 40 mm. Should the two sensor holes move apart? Answer: Not with our intent. Their pitch comes from the sensor interface, so both remain on the same centerline at ±12 mm.

Prediction: A sketch is fully constrained, but its dimensions use an incorrect measurement. Is it ready to print? Answer: Constraint completeness only describes freedom inside the model. The measured interface still needs verification.

Prediction: A 0.1 mm layer height means the hole diameter is accurate to 0.1 mm. Answer: No. Vertical slicing resolution does not establish in-plane dimensional accuracy, shrinkage, or repeatability.

Your next independent part

Choose a real lightweight sensor, switch, or cable guide. Measure its interfaces twice, identify an assembly direction, and model a mount using no more than eight meaningful parameters. Produce two size variants without rebuilding the sketch. Ask another person to identify the fixed hardware dimensions from your parameter names and drawing.

Keep the part on the bench until its fit and fastening method are checked. Your deliverable is a clear model, a change test, and a list of uncertainties. The 3D-printing chapter turns those uncertainties into small, measurable trials.

Choose a material and a way to make it

A bracket can fail without breaking. It may bend enough to point a sensor in the wrong direction, slowly relax under a screw, swell after absorbing moisture, or soften near a motor. “Strong plastic” does not describe those requirements well enough to choose a material.

This chapter gives you a practical way to connect a part’s job to its material, shape, and manufacturing process. You need the earlier measurement ideas, plus multiplication and unit conversion. CAD familiarity helps with later design comparisons but is optional for the card-strip lab. By the end, you will be able to explain a material choice, recognize missing evidence in a datasheet, and run a modest comparison without mistaking it for a certified test.

Translate the job into property questions

Start with the environment and the failure you want to prevent. A bench sensor mount may need to hold its angle for a month, survive occasional handling, and accept repeated assembly. A protective cover may care more about impact and appearance. A bearing seat may depend on dimensional stability and local contact pressure.

Separate properties that everyday language tends to combine:

PropertyQuestion it answersExample consequence
StiffnessHow much does it deform elastically under load?A sensor shifts despite an unbroken mount
StrengthWhat stress produces a specified failure or permanent deformation?A rib yields or fractures
ToughnessHow much energy can it absorb before fracture?A dropped cover cracks
HardnessHow resistant is its surface to localized indentation?A washer dents the mounting face
Creep resistanceHow well does it resist time-dependent deformation under sustained load?A clamped polymer slowly changes shape
Fatigue resistanceHow does it tolerate repeated loading?A vibrating root develops a crack

These properties are related but not interchangeable. A material can be stiff and brittle. A flexible material can survive large deformation while being unsuitable for accurate positioning. A short pull test cannot establish months of creep performance.

Temperature and time belong in the requirement, not in an afterthought. “Supports 200 g” is incomplete if the mount sits in direct sunlight or carries that load for years. Specify the orientation, duration, temperature range, and acceptable motion. For more depth, MIT’s Mechanical Behavior of Materials connects elasticity, deformation, creep, and fracture to material mechanisms. It is an undergraduate technical course, best approached after basic mechanics rather than used as a first shopping guide.

The process is part of the material

A material name alone does not define the object you manufacture. A molded polymer specimen and an FDM part made from a related polymer can differ in voids, bonding, orientation, and residual stress. Even two prints from one spool may differ if their layer orientation or thermal history changes.

Anisotropy means properties depend on direction. Wood has grain. Fiber composites have reinforcing directions. FDM parts have deposited roads and layer interfaces. A force that mainly stretches roads can create a different failure mode from a force that tends to separate layers. “Print it stronger” therefore begins by identifying the load direction and the weak interfaces.

Infill percentage does not turn a print into a homogeneous material with a proportionally reduced strength. Perimeters, skins, infill connections, and local geometry form a structure. A bolt hole surrounded by several continuous walls behaves differently from one crossing a sparse interior. The material label and the slicer settings must travel together in your build record.

Metal stock also has process history. Alloy, temper or heat treatment, and product form matter. An unidentified piece of aluminum is not equivalent to a certified grade simply because its density looks familiar. Plywood depends on veneer construction and moisture; acrylic and polycarbonate should not be grouped as “clear sheet” when impact behavior matters.

The useful decision is therefore a package: material grade + stock form + manufacturing process + geometry + inspection.

Worked example: geometry changes the material comparison

Consider a solid rectangular prototype arm 100 mm long, 20 mm wide, and 4 mm thick. Its volume is:

V = 100 × 20 × 4 = 8,000 mm³ = 8 cm³

Using illustrative densities of 1.25 g/cm³ for a polymer and 2.70 g/cm³ for an aluminum alloy gives masses of 10.0 g and 21.6 g. These are arithmetic assumptions, not measured values for your spool or stock. An FDM part with cavities will contain less material than the solid CAD volume, and supports add material that does not remain in the finished part.

Now imagine bending through the 4 mm thickness. For a rectangular section, the second moment of area is I = b h³ / 12, where h is the dimension in the bending direction. Doubling thickness from 4 to 8 mm multiplies I by eight while doubling volume. Doubling width from 20 to 40 mm multiplies both I and volume by two.

Shape is part of the material decision. This ideal section comparison does not include joints or print direction.

For the same length and loading, beam deflection is inversely proportional to the product E I: material modulus times geometric second moment of area. This explains why changing shape can be more effective than choosing an expensive filament. It also explains why a thin sheet can feel floppy in one direction and rigid when turned on edge.

There is no free gain. A deeper arm occupies more space, alters connections, and may become difficult to print or machine. A hollow section needs enough wall stability and suitable load introduction at its ends. Remove material only after considering where the force enters, travels, and leaves.

Michael Ashby’s Materials Selection in Mechanical Design develops this material–shape–process reasoning systematically. It is a paid university-level book; start with selection basics and process effects before the more mathematical optimization chapters.

Read a datasheet as an experiment report

When comparing two listed strengths, ask how each number was obtained. Look for specimen process, orientation, conditioning, temperature, test method, and whether the value is typical or guaranteed. A tensile modulus does not directly supply impact toughness. A heat-deflection result under a specified test load is not a universal continuous service temperature for your geometry.

For filament, prefer data that identifies printed specimen conditions when evaluating a printed part. If a table only describes raw resin, record that limitation. Short carbon fibers can change stiffness, dimensional behavior, surface finish, and processing requirements; the word “carbon” alone does not establish better layer adhesion or stronger joints.

Manufacturer material guides help narrow candidates. Prusa’s filament material guide compares printing and application characteristics across materials, but its settings belong to its documented process context. For a Bambu printer, confirm nozzle compatibility, drying guidance, build surface, and temperature settings against the actual filament and printer instructions.

Use a decision table with three columns: requirement, evidence, and unresolved question. “Must hold its angle indoors” might have an initial stiffness estimate and a planned sustained-load observation. “Must survive a hot car” requires a different evidence trail. Do not silently turn an unknown into a pass.

Lab: separate shape effects from time effects

Allow an hour for setup and observation. You need thin card, scissors, a ruler, tape, two small paper clips, and a flat table. An optional second stage uses a spare thin printed strip. Keep loads close to the table, away from eyes and fragile objects. Do not use heat or bend specimens toward your face.

Cut two card strips approximately 120 × 20 mm. Keep one flat. Fold the other lengthwise into an L-shaped section without cutting it. Tape 40 mm of each strip securely to the table so 80 mm projects beyond the edge; support them only a few centimeters above a clear surface if possible.

First observe both under their own weight. Then attach the same small paper clip at each free end. Use a ruler placed beside the tip to record the change in height. Photograph from the side to reduce parallax. Do not keep adding load until something breaks; this experiment compares response at modest deformation.

Expect the folded strip to resist vertical bending more effectively because the fold distributes material over a greater section depth. Your actual result depends on the fold, tape compliance, and card grain. If the folded strip twists, the load may be off-center or the open section may be responding in torsion. If both strips move at the tape, you are measuring the fixture as well as the strips.

For the optional time observation, load a thin spare printed strip only enough to produce a small visible deflection. Record immediately, after ten minutes, and after an hour; then remove the load and observe recovery. Record room conditions and the print orientation. You may see little change at this load and duration. That does not prove creep is absent over longer periods or at higher temperature.

A no-material alternative is to predict the two card responses with sketches and compare their section depths. The learning target is to separate material, shape, fixture behavior, and time. These are proposed observations, not laboratory results already established for the pivot.

Check your reasoning

Prediction: A polymer arm is too flexible. Would a higher tensile-strength label guarantee less deflection? Answer: No. Elastic modulus and section geometry determine small elastic deflection; strength describes a different limit.

Prediction: The same strip is twice as thick, with length and width unchanged. What happens to solid mass and ideal bending stiffness? Answer: Mass doubles and I increases eightfold. Actual printed structure and joint compliance may prevent the complete assembly from improving by eightfold.

Prediction: A bolt stayed tight during a ten-minute test. Have you checked long-term clamp stability in plastic? Answer: No. Sustained contact pressure and changing temperature can alter the joint over time. Longer observation and an appropriate joint design are still needed.

Choose a route for your own mount

Take the real part you modeled in CAD, or use a dimensioned sketch if you have not completed that chapter. Compare an FDM version, a stock-material version, and a purchased component. State one condition under which each would be the better choice. Include total effort: sourcing, setup, finishing, inspection, and replacement, not just grams of material.

It has not been physically printed or load-tested. Design the print, then inspect the toolpaths

A solid model describes the volume you want. A slicer describes the roads of plastic that attempt to create it. Those are different objects. A thin rib can exist in CAD yet disappear in the slice. A smooth overhang can become a staircase with little support beneath each new layer. A circular hole can print smaller than its nominal diameter.

Your task is to connect geometry, orientation, extrusion paths, and measured fit. This chapter assumes you can sketch and extrude a simple part. You will build a small fit coupon, inspect it layer by layer, and decide what to change from evidence. A printer is optional for the first half; Bambu Studio can prepare and preview a project without a physical print. The official Bambu Studio project identifies its slicing, G-code viewing, support, and project features.

Think in deposited roads

In fused-filament fabrication, a hot nozzle deposits plastic in successive layers. Within one layer, the slicer typically creates boundary walls, interior fill, and solid surface regions. The nozzle diameter constrains useful extrusion widths, but it does not force every line to equal that diameter. Modern slicers can vary line widths, and adjacent roads overlap according to the extrusion model.

Layer height sets vertical sampling. Reducing it can improve sloped-surface stair steps, but it does not automatically improve a vertical hole’s in-plane size. A tall vertical wall may look smoother while its diameter error remains unchanged. Prusa’s layers and perimeters reference is useful for understanding the distinction between layer height, shell paths, and thin features; setting names differ between slicers.

A CAD wall 1.2 mm thick does not guarantee exactly three 0.4 mm paths. The slicer must fit its chosen widths and spacing into that region. It may create variable-width walls, gap fill, or a different count than you expected. Inspect the result at the narrowest point, especially where a diagonal brace joins a node.

Likewise, increasing infill may do little for a member already filled almost entirely by walls. For bending, outer material often matters greatly because stress grows with distance from the neutral axis. For bolt contact, local solid material and continuous wall paths may matter more than distant infill. Strength settings should answer a structural question.

Choose orientation by several consequences

A reasonable orientation balances at least four concerns: load direction, support access, critical surface quality, and bed stability. Optimizing only print time can create an awkward or weak interface.

Consider an L-shaped sensor bracket loaded at the end of its upright leg. Standing it in the same orientation as its use may put tension across layer interfaces near the corner. Laying it on a side may give more continuous roads through that corner, while changing the quality of its holes and contact faces. Neither choice is universally best; draw the load and examine the layer direction at the stressed region.

Overhangs require the new road to be sufficiently supported by material below, or by a designed bridge or support structure. The familiar “45-degree rule” is a starting heuristic, not a guarantee. Cooling, material, layer height, line width, speed, and local geometry change the outcome. A short bridge anchored at both ends differs from a broad cantilevered ceiling.

Supports also need an exit route. A cavity that a slicer can fill with supports may be impossible to clean without damaging the part. Keep bearing seats and mating faces away from support contact when practical, or plan a finishing operation and allow for it. Prusa’s modeling guide discusses orientation, overhangs, tolerances, and splitting parts as connected design decisions.

Chamfers and fillets have specific jobs

A small chamfer at a bed-contact edge can provide relief for first-layer spreading, often called elephant foot. It removes nominal material locally so the bottom lip is less likely to interfere with assembly. It does not repair an incorrectly calibrated first layer, and too much relief reduces the contact footprint.

A chamfer at a hole entrance can help a shaft or screw find the opening. Its size must leave enough cylindrical engagement and enough material around the hole. A fillet at a structural rib root can soften a stress concentration, but a rounded underside may introduce a difficult shallow overhang near the bed. The same shape can help mechanics and complicate manufacturing.

For the 4 mm-thick exercise coupon below, begin with a 0.3 mm bed-edge chamfer and a 0.5 mm entrance chamfer as trial geometry. These values are intentionally small enough to study on a coupon; they are not verified settings for every printer or every pivot interface. Keep an unchamfered comparison if you want to isolate the effect.

Do not bevel every bearing-seat edge. A bearing needs an adequately long cylindrical seat and a shoulder that contacts the intended race. Unplanned edge removal can change axial location or reduce support. Apply an edge treatment only after stating the surface’s function.

Worked example: a clearance ladder

Suppose the smooth pin you intend to fit measures 7.98 mm. You want a freely removable fit, but you do not yet know the printed-hole bias. Model holes at 8.10, 8.20, 8.30, 8.40, and 8.50 mm. Their nominal diametral clearances relative to this pin are:

8.10 − 7.98 = 0.12 mm
8.20 − 7.98 = 0.22 mm
8.30 − 7.98 = 0.32 mm
8.40 − 7.98 = 0.42 mm
8.50 − 7.98 = 0.52 mm

If the first hole actually prints at 7.95 mm, it has 0.03 mm interference despite the nominal clearance. That example is hypothetical: measure or functionally gauge your own result. Small-hole caliper readings can be unreliable because the jaw tips and alignment affect contact. A measured smooth pin is a useful fit gauge, but it does not reveal an exact diameter or roundness by itself.

CAM begins with how the work is held

A milling machine can follow a toolpath accurately and still make the wrong part. The stock may be located differently from the digital setup. A clamp may sit in the cutter’s path. The last contour may release the part before machining is finished. These are planning errors that attractive CAM animation can conceal.

This chapter teaches the reasoning between a CAD model and a manufacturing setup. You need the CAD and measurement chapters and basic unit arithmetic. You will make a paper workholding plan and, optionally, a simulated CAM setup. You will not generate a machine-ready operating recipe. Actual machining requires training on the specific machine, tooling, controller, and shop procedures.

A manufacturing plan has several coordinate systems

Start with the finished part, then identify the stock from which it will be made. A 50 × 30 × 4 mm plate cannot simply be held by empty space while every outer surface is cut. The stock may need extra thickness, a larger border, or a fixture so the cutter can reach the required faces while the part remains restrained.

CAD coordinates describe the model. A work coordinate system, or WCS, relates the program to the physical workpiece. The machine also has its own coordinates, established through its machine-specific reference procedure. Tool offsets account for the actual tool geometry and its location in the holder.

These systems must agree, but they are not interchangeable. Moving the digital model to an attractive place in the viewport does not establish a real work offset. Changing the selected top surface in CAM can alter where the program expects Z = 0. The operator must be able to identify and establish the same reference physically.

For a simple planning exercise, choose the top of the raw stock as Z = 0 and one identifiable stock corner as the XY origin. Draw arrows for positive X, Y, and Z. If the stock top is later faced away, decide whether subsequent operations retain the original reference or use a newly established finished surface. Record the decision instead of relying on memory.

Tool access changes the design

A rotating end mill has a finite diameter. It cannot create an arbitrarily sharp internal corner in an ordinary planar pocket. A 6 mm cutter leaves an internal radius of at least roughly 3 mm in that cutting plane, subject to the actual tool geometry and path. If a square mating tab must fit, you can round the tab's corners to clear that radius, add deliberate relief to the pocket, enlarge the pocket where the fit allows, or choose another process. Simply increasing the pocket's internal corner radius leaves more material in its corners and can worsen interference with an unchanged square tab.

Smaller tools can reach tighter geometry, but are generally less rigid and may require different engagement, speed, and feed choices. Longer reach also increases deflection and collision concerns. The cutter tip reaching a pocket does not prove the shank or holder clears its walls.

Distinguish through holes, blind holes, pockets, slots, and external contours. Their tool access and inspection differ. A blind pocket must leave a known floor thickness. A through cut needs a plan for the material beneath it and the part’s retention. A deep narrow slot may be disproportionately difficult compared with a small redesign.

Machining drawings should make important functional surfaces clear. Unnecessary tight tolerances and polished finishes can add operations without improving function. Conversely, a bearing seat or a pair of locating holes may require careful control even when the rest of the part is rough.

Workholding is a force problem

The cutter exerts forces on the stock. The fixture must resist translation, rotation, and lifting without distorting the part or obstructing the tool. Friction alone is not a complete explanation unless you know the clamping force, contact conditions, and cutting loads.

Think through the force path: cutter into stock, stock into supports or locating faces, clamps into the fixture, fixture into the machine table. A thin plate can bow under a clamp and spring back after release. The machine then produces a surface that is accurate only while the part is distorted.

Separate locating from clamping. Locators define where the part sits; clamps hold it against those references. The familiar 3–2–1 principle illustrates how three contacts on a primary plane, two on a second plane, and one on a third can constrain a rigid body. Real fixtures must also account for contact area, debris, accessibility, and cutting forces.

Do not overinterpret a schematic. Six carefully drawn dots do not prove a real fixture is rigid, secure, or safe. Local shop review determines an appropriate setup. Never hand-hold work near a rotating cutter, and do not treat a hand-held object as a substitute for approved drill-press workholding.

Worked example: feed, speed, and units

Three quantities often get confused:

  • Spindle speed is rotation rate, usually revolutions per minute.
  • Cutting speed is the speed of the cutting edge relative to the material, often meters per minute.
  • Feed rate is the programmed advance, often millimeters per minute.

For a purely arithmetic example, assume a 6 mm-diameter cutter, two effective teeth, 6,000 rpm, and 0.020 mm advance per tooth. These are invented calculation inputs, not recommended cutting conditions for any material or machine.

feed = feed per tooth × teeth × revolutions per minute
     = 0.020 mm/tooth × 2 teeth/rev × 6,000 rev/min
     = 240 mm/min

cutting speed = π × diameter × rpm / 1,000
              = π × 6 mm × 6,000 / 1,000
              ≈ 113.1 m/min

The division by 1,000 converts millimeters to meters. If a trained setup plan kept feed per tooth unchanged while doubling rpm, feed would also double. Doubling rpm while leaving feed unchanged halves the nominal feed per tooth. Neither alteration is automatically appropriate: tool engagement, chip evacuation, runout, workpiece material, and machine capability also matter.

Sandvik Coromant’s milling formulas and definitions is a primary reference for these quantities. Use manufacturer cutting data with the correct tool and operation under qualified supervision. A formula converts chosen inputs; it does not choose safe inputs for you.

From setup to toolpath to machine code

In CAM, a setup typically identifies the model, stock, orientation, and work origin. Operations then describe how tools remove material: facing, roughing a pocket, drilling, finishing a wall, or contouring the outside. Their order matters because each operation changes the stock available to support the next.

Roughing removes bulk material while leaving a controlled allowance. Finishing aims at final surfaces and dimensions. “Leave 0.3 mm” means something only if you know whether it applies radially, axially, or both. Entry, exit, retract, clearance, and linking moves deserve the same attention as cutting moves: a rapid motion can collide with a clamp even if the cutting contour is correct.

A post processor translates the CAM operations into code for a particular controller and machine configuration. It is not a generic export button. Tool changes, work offsets, arcs, and other commands can be interpreted differently across systems. A file extension alone does not establish compatibility.

Simulation can reveal stock left behind and some collisions, especially when tools, holders, fixtures, and machine geometry are represented correctly. It cannot validate an omitted clamp, an incorrectly measured tool, weak workholding, or the true behavior of a flexible part. Review warnings and compare the simulation assumptions with the physical setup.

Its machine-oriented content should support supervised instruction; it does not authorize unsupervised operation of another shop’s equipment.

Follow the force before strengthening the part

Builds on02 · Math and physics at the workbench03 · Measure, experiment and debug06 · Materials and manufacturing choices

A robot module hanging from a joint does more than pull downward. Its weight acts at a distance, creating a moment. The joint transfers that moment through bearings, shaft, housing, fasteners, and the neighboring structure. Improving one piece can simply move the weakest point elsewhere.

This chapter teaches you to trace that load path, draw a free-body diagram, and use simple calculations to compare designs. You need units, basic algebra, and the materials chapter. The examples use idealized structures to explain trends. They do not establish a payload rating for a printed joint.

Draw the object you are analyzing

A free-body diagram isolates one object or assembly and replaces its contacts with external forces and moments. Draw the boundary first. If you isolate the complete robot module, internal bearing forces do not belong on that diagram. If you isolate its shaft, bearing reactions do.

For a stationary object, the net force and net moment must balance. In a two-dimensional problem, this usually gives three equilibrium equations: horizontal forces sum to zero, vertical forces sum to zero, and moments sum to zero. Choose a positive direction and keep signs consistent. A negative answer tells you the actual force acts opposite your assumed arrow.

A moment is force times perpendicular distance from a reference point to the force’s line of action. A 2 N downward force acting 100 mm away produces 200 N·mm = 0.20 N·m. The perpendicular distance matters; using a slanted member’s full length can give the wrong result.

The freely available Engineering Statics: Open & Interactive provides diagrams and exercises for forces, equilibrium, structures, and moments of area. Start with free-body diagrams and moments before attempting a complicated assembly. The author-linked statics videos offer a complementary visual explanation; pause to draw your own arrows before watching a solution.

Two bearings resist a moment through a force pair

Two separated bearing stations can react an overturning moment because they apply forces at different positions along the shaft. The important distance is their center-to-center spacing along the bearing axis. Two bearings pressed immediately beside one another do not provide the same leverage as widely separated stations.

Consider an ideal shaft with bearing A at X = 0 and bearing B at X = 25.1 mm. Apply a 2 N downward force at X = 125.1 mm: the force is 100 mm beyond bearing B. Ignore shaft weight and assume the bearings act as simple radial supports.

The arrows are forces on the isolated shaft. The housing receives the opposite forces.

Taking moments about A gives:

R_B × 25.1 − 2 × 125.1 = 0
R_B = 250.2 / 25.1 ≈ 9.97 N upward

R_A + R_B − 2 = 0
R_A ≈ −7.97 N

Bearing A therefore pushes downward on the isolated shaft while B pushes upward. The shaft applies equal and opposite forces to the bearings and housing. The supports see forces several times the 2 N external load because the load is overhung.

Another way to see it is a direct 2 N reaction plus a force pair reacting the 2 × 100 = 200 N·mm overhang moment. The pair magnitude is 200/25.1 ≈ 7.97 N. Increasing station spacing reduces this component for the same applied moment, but shaft length, packaging, alignment, and housing stiffness also change.

This example explains the role of spacing in the triangulated pivot, which uses two 608 bearings with 25.1 mm center spacing and an 8 mm shaft. Its actual load geometry is more complicated. The calculation is not a bearing-life calculation, a shaft check, or a validation of its printed seats. That assembly has not been physically printed, assembled, or load-tested.

Also distinguish overturning moment from torque about the rotation axis. Bearings intended to allow rotation do not hold the joint at an angle by themselves. A brake, actuator, detent, or other restraint is needed if the mechanism must resist rotation about its own free axis.

Stress and stiffness answer different questions

Average direct stress is force divided by load-carrying area. With force in newtons and area in square millimeters, the result is N/mm², numerically equal to MPa. A 20 N tensile force through 10 mm² produces an average stress of 2 MPa.

“Average” matters. Holes, abrupt corners, notches, and contact points create local concentrations. A bearing seat or bolt hole may also experience contact pressure, splitting, or bending that a simple force-over-area calculation misses. Local wall thickness and how load spreads into the structure matter.

Stiffness describes deformation under load. A part can remain below its failure stress while bending too much to function. For an ideal straight cantilever beam with a tip force, constant rectangular section, linear elastic material, and small deflection:

I = b h³ / 12
maximum bending stress = F L (h/2) / I
tip deflection = F L³ / (3 E I)

Here b is width, h is depth in the bending direction, L is unsupported length, and E is Young’s modulus. The second moment of area I has units of mm⁴; it describes section geometry, not mass.

Take a 40 mm-long arm, 15 mm wide and 3 mm deep, with a 2 N tip load. Assume E = 2,000 N/mm² solely for the example:

I = 15 × 3³ / 12 = 33.75 mm⁴
root moment = 2 × 40 = 80 N·mm
maximum stress = 80 × 1.5 / 33.75 ≈ 3.56 MPa
deflection = 2 × 40³ / (3 × 2,000 × 33.75)
           ≈ 0.632 mm

Doubling depth to 6 mm multiplies I by eight. The maximum bending stress falls by four, because the outer-fiber distance also doubles; deflection falls by eight. Doubling length instead doubles the bending stress and multiplies deflection by eight. Long, thin arms can become frustratingly flexible before they look close to breaking.

These formulas assume a rigid clamp and a homogeneous beam. FDM directionality, sparse interiors, flexible joints, and large deformation can invalidate that model. MIT’s Mechanics & Materials I develops the underlying relationships. Its university sequence expects classical mechanics and substantial mathematics; use its notes as a deeper path, not a prerequisite for the small experiment below.

Why triangles help, and where they stop helping

A rectangle made from four ideal pin-connected bars can change into a parallelogram without changing bar lengths. Add a diagonal and that easy motion disappears: changing the shape now requires stretching, compressing, or otherwise deforming members.

That is the basic attraction of triangulation. An ideal truss carries loads primarily as axial tension and compression when loads enter at joints and the joints behave as pins. A printed lattice is usually a frame with continuous nodes, finite member widths, and some bending. Calling it a truss does not make the ideal assumptions true.

For an octagonal housing, triangulating side faces can reduce racking, but the load must also enter and leave through well-supported ends. A strong diagonal ending at a thin ring can concentrate deformation there. A bearing-support face needs paths from the hub to the outer structure, sufficient node area, and clearance for assembly.

Compression introduces buckling. A long slender member can bow sideways at a load much lower than its crushing strength. For an ideal slender elastic column, Euler’s result is P_cr = π² E I / (K L)², where the effective-length factor K represents end restraint. Doubling length quarters this ideal buckling load. Real imperfections, eccentric loading, printed surfaces, and uncertain end restraint often reduce performance. Do not apply the formula blindly to short blocks or assume a printed node is perfectly fixed.

Repeated loads and safety factors

A mechanism sees more than one load case. List gravity in different orientations, cable pull, acceleration, contact with a stop, handling, and repeated motion. A brief impact can exceed a static weight estimate. Repeated stresses can initiate and grow fatigue cracks, especially near notches, joints, and defects.

A factor of safety compares a justified failure limit with the predicted demand for a stated failure mode. For example, if an applicable failure stress were 12 MPa and a valid model predicted 4 MPa, their ratio would be three. An allowable design stress may already include a required safety factor; dividing that allowable by demand describes reserve against the allowable, so do not count the original factor twice. The word “justified” carries much of the work: a catalog resin strength is not automatically an applicable limit for a printed, notched, aging component.

A large numerical factor does not repair the wrong load direction, omitted buckling, uncertain attachment, or a weak support. Different failure modes need different checks. Strength, stiffness, fit retention, fatigue, and bearing life do not collapse into one number. Early prototypes should be tested at low energy with a clearly limited purpose rather than advertised with a load rating.

Lab: triangulate a frame and observe the whole system

Turn the frame over or reverse the push so the diagonal changes from tension to compression.

Draw a free-body diagram of one diagonal for the direction where it is in tension. At its two ends, draw forces along the member, pulling outward. Then sketch the compression case with inward forces. This is a deliberately simplified model; the card and taped joints also bend.

Measure unloading as well as loading. Recovery, residual deflection, and changing response across repeated trials tell you more than whether the part survived once. Your deliverable is a test plan and an evidence record with limits—not an unsupported claim that a new lattice is strong enough.

Mechanisms and actuators: make the motion earn its motor

Builds on02 · Math and physics at the workbench09 · Forces, structures and failure

Imagine a small sensor on a tabletop arm. You want to turn it toward a target, hold it still while taking a reading, then return to a known position. A motor can provide rotation, but it does not decide how the arm is supported, how much it bends, or whether a command corresponds to the angle you actually obtain. Those questions belong to the whole mechanism.

This chapter turns a desired motion into a first transmission and actuator specification. You will calculate torque and speed, distinguish several causes of positioning error, and use a hand-operated experiment to test a load model. Bring the force, moment, and free-body-diagram ideas from the mechanics chapter. No electronics or motor purchase is needed for the core lab. The numbers here describe a separate teaching example; they do not establish an actuator rating for the untested triangulated pivot.

Begin at the output

Describe what the load must do before choosing a motor. Our illustrative arm carries a 100 g sensor, with its center of mass 80 mm from a horizontal rotation axis. It must sweep through 90° in two seconds and stop. Write down the orientation: a vertical axis does not experience the same gravitational turning moment as a horizontal one. Also record travel limits, acceptable error, operating time, and what should happen when power disappears.

Mass is not force. A 0.100 kg sensor has weight approximately 0.100 × 9.81 = 0.981 N. When the arm is horizontal, the perpendicular distance from the axis to the weight's line of action is 0.080 m. The gravitational torque is therefore:

τ = F × r = 0.981 × 0.080 = 0.0785 N·m

A newton metre, N·m, is force multiplied by perpendicular distance. If the arm turns upward, that perpendicular distance shrinks. The worst gravitational case is the horizontal position, not every position. The arm's own weight, cable drag, friction, and acceleration add demands that this first calculation omits. Add them as identifiable terms; a large unexplained multiplier hides what you still need to measure.

For example, a uniform 40 g arm extending 80 mm contributes another 0.040 × 9.81 × 0.040 = 0.0157 N·m when horizontal. The combined static requirement becomes 0.0942 N·m. That is a holding requirement at one configuration. A mechanism intended to move quickly also needs torque to change its angular speed. Rotational inertia describes resistance to angular acceleration; mass farther from the axis contributes disproportionately. Moving the same sensor closer to the axis can reduce both gravity torque and acceleration demand.

A transmission trades distance and speed for force

A reduction gear makes the motor turn several revolutions for one output revolution. Define the reduction ratio here as G = motor speed / output speed. With a 20:1 reduction, 600 rpm at the motor becomes 30 rpm at the output. In an ideal transmission, output torque is twenty times motor torque. Real transmissions dissipate energy, so use an efficiency estimate, η, between zero and one:

output torque ≈ motor torque × G × η

Suppose our preliminary moving-load target is 0.15 N·m, including explicitly provisional allowances for friction and acceleration. At G = 20 and assumed η = 0.70, motor torque must be approximately 0.15 / (20 × 0.70) = 0.0107 N·m. The efficiency is a design assumption to verify, not a property shared by every gearbox.

The sweep's average output speed is 45°/s, or 7.5 rpm. A smooth start and stop require a higher peak speed than that average. If we provisionally choose 15 rpm peak output speed, the motor must deliver its required torque at about 300 rpm. A listing that separately advertises enough torque and enough speed does not prove that both are available simultaneously.

Power makes the trade clearer. Mechanical power is P = τω, with angular speed ω in radians per second. One revolution is 2π radians; 15 rpm is 15 × 2π / 60 = 1.571 rad/s. Delivering 0.15 N·m at that speed takes approximately 0.236 W mechanically. Reduction changes torque and speed together; it does not create power. Losses increase the electrical input required.

Gears are one option. A toothed belt can move the motor away from the load and provide reduction with widely spaced pulleys. A lead screw converts rotation to translation: a 2 mm lead advances 2 mm per revolution, so 120 rpm gives 4 mm/s. A linkage can produce a useful path or mechanical advantage that changes through its motion. These choices also change packaging, friction, stiffness, maintenance, and failure behavior. Do not assume a screw or gearbox will hold a load without power; back-driving behavior depends on geometry, friction, wear, and operating conditions.

Three errors that feel similar

Turn an output clockwise to a mark, then approach the same mark counterclockwise. If the input moves before the output responds, clearance between mating parts creates backlash. If the output moves under load and springs back when released, the mechanism has compliance, the inverse of stiffness. If it sticks and then jumps, static friction and changing contact forces are involved. A higher-resolution encoder does not remove any of these mechanical causes.

Accuracy describes closeness to a desired value. Repeatability describes how tightly repeated attempts cluster. A rig might repeatedly stop 2° short: repeatable, but inaccurate. It might average the correct angle while scattering widely: correct average, poor repeatability. Record the direction of approach and applied load when measuring either property.

Support the load through an intentional bearing or guide arrangement. A motor shaft may have limited allowable radial and axial loads; its ability to produce torque does not establish its ability to support an overhung arm. A flexible coupling accommodates specified misalignment between supported shafts. It is not a substitute for supporting a loose shaft. Revisit the free-body diagram with the transmission installed: gear and belt forces add loads of their own.

What the motor can actually deliver

A brushed DC motor develops torque through current in its windings. As it turns, it generates a voltage opposing the supply, called back electromotive force, or back EMF. At rest that opposing voltage is absent, so starting current can be large. A blocked motor also remains in that condition. Manufacturer guidance distinguishes free-running current, stall current, and controller limits for this reason. Pololu's motor and supply selection guide is a useful practical treatment.

For an illustrative motor at one fixed supply voltage, suppose no-load speed is 1,000 rpm and stall torque is 0.050 N·m. A simple straight-line approximation predicts 800 rpm at 0.010 N·m and 400 rpm at 0.030 N·m. This is a teaching model, not a purchased motor specification. Obtain a real torque–speed curve, winding limits, gearbox limits, and operating-duty information before selecting hardware.

At stall, speed is zero, so useful mechanical power τω is zero even though electrical power is entering and heating the motor. Stall torque is consequently a poor continuous-duty target. A motor that survives a brief startup may overheat if held against a stop. Gear teeth or a coupling may fail before the motor does. Never perform an unrestricted stall test to discover the rating of an unknown motor.

A servo is a feedback-controlled actuator system: a controller compares measured motion with the requested motion and changes the drive. A hobby servo commonly packages motor, gears, position sensing, and controller, but its advertised torque still needs supply, thermal, and duty-cycle context. A stepper motor advances through commanded magnetic states. Counting steps can predict position only while the rotor follows those commands; excessive load or acceleration can cause missed steps. Holding torque at zero speed does not describe torque at operating speed. Adafruit's stepper guide provides an accessible introduction and a separate driver-matching discussion.

The driver belongs in the specification

A microcontroller output is a signal, not a motor power source. A motor driver switches current from a suitable supply. A reversible DC motor typically uses an H-bridge; a stepper needs appropriate phase control and, commonly, regulated winding current. Select the driver using motor voltage, startup and stall behavior, current limiting, heat dissipation, and fault response. A brief peak-current number is not the same as a continuous rating on your actual board.

Pulse-width modulation, PWM, switches power on and off quickly; duty cycle is the fraction of each period spent on. It controls average behavior but does not make every electrical or thermal quantity equal to its duty-scaled value. Inductance, switching frequency, recirculation paths, and load matter. Use the driver's documented wiring and supply range rather than treating PWM as permission to exceed a motor's rating.

Plan a limit switch or position reference, a maximum permitted motion time, and a physical way to remove drive power. Arrange the mechanism so a software crash cannot unexpectedly drop a load onto a person. For a first powered trial, use a restrained, low-energy mechanism and an accessible power disconnect, keep fingers outside pinch points, and limit current according to the chosen hardware. These are part of designing the experiment, not features to add after it moves.

Lab: measure the lever before selecting its actuator

Use a light ruler or stiff cardboard strip, pencil, tape, two equal small objects, and a tabletop. Coins or identical washers work; use only small masses and keep everything just above a tray or table so nothing can fall far. A scale is optional. This is a balance experiment, not a strength test of a printed joint.

  1. Mark the strip's center. Balance the empty strip on the pencil at that mark. Adjust the pivot until the strip's own weight produces approximately no turning moment.
  2. Tape one object 40 mm to the left of the pivot. Place an equal object on the right and find its balance distance. Predict 40 mm before adjusting it.
  3. Replace the right-hand object with two equal objects. Predict a 20 mm balance distance, then test. Record actual distances from the pivot to the objects' approximate centers of mass.
  4. Keep the two objects on the right but move them outward by 5 mm. Describe which side falls and explain the change with a torque equation.
  5. Draw the load path through strip, pivot, and tabletop. Identify a force that the torque balance alone did not tell you: the pivot's upward support reaction.

Expected observations: equal weights approximately balance at equal distances; twice the weight approximately balances at half the distance. A thick pencil contact, tape mass, nonuniform ruler, and uncertain object centers can shift the result. If balancing depends strongly on which side you approach from, suspect pivot friction before changing the torque law. These are predicted observations; this handbook does not claim that this physical lab has been performed.

Now write the arm example's actuator requirement on paper: travel, peak speed assumption, gravity torque, acceleration allowance, duty cycle, support arrangement, and response to power loss. Mark each entry as calculated, assumed, specified, or measured. A usable specification makes uncertainty visible.

DC circuits: predict the reading before touching the probe

Builds on02 · Math and physics at the workbench03 · Measure, experiment and debug

A sensor logger needs an electrical signal that represents something in the world. Before adding a sensor, build a signal whose value you can predict: two resistors dividing a small supply voltage. This modest circuit introduces the ideas behind power wiring, sensor interfaces, fault finding, and eventually circuit boards.

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to name a circuit's nodes, calculate currents and power, explain why a measurement can change a circuit, and check a breadboard without using a meter as an accidental short circuit. You need arithmetic, unit prefixes, and the habit of writing a prediction before measuring. The core calculations and software lab require no purchased hardware. The optional bench route uses only a current-limited 3.0 V supply.

Voltage belongs between two points

Electric current is the rate at which charge passes a location. Its unit is the ampere, A; one milliampere, mA, is 0.001 A, and one microampere, µA, is 0.000001 A. Voltage is an electrical potential difference, measured in volts, V. Informally, it describes the energy available per unit charge moving between two points. A voltmeter therefore always compares two points, even when people casually say “the voltage at this pin.”

We will call the supply's positive node SUPPLY, the shared reference node GND, and the resistor junction MID. A node is a set of points connected by conductors that we treat as having the same voltage. An ideal wire has no voltage drop; a real wire has resistance and other properties that become important later. Ground in this little isolated circuit is our chosen zero-volt reference. It is not automatically the building's protective earth.

Connect resistor R1 from SUPPLY to MID and resistor R2 from MID to GND. The supply connects between GND and SUPPLY. Current has a complete loop: through the source, R1, R2, and back to the source. Removing either resistor breaks this loop. The circuit diagram records electrical connections, not the physical arrangement of parts on the bench.

Conventional current in the external resistor path goes from higher to lower potential. Electrons in metal move the other way; either description is consistent when used correctly. We use conventional current throughout this handbook. Charge is not used up by a resistor: energy is transferred, while the steady current entering a two-terminal resistor equals the current leaving it.

One component law and two accounting laws

For an ideal resistor, Ohm's law relates voltage across it, current through it, and resistance: V = IR. Resistance is measured in ohms, Ω. A 10 kΩ resistor has 10,000 Ω resistance. At a 1 V drop, it carries 1 / 10,000 = 0.0001 A, or 100 µA. The law models a resistor over its operating conditions; it is not a universal description of LEDs, motors, or every component bearing two leads.

Kirchhoff's current law says that current entering a node equals current leaving it. It is charge accounting. Kirchhoff's voltage law says that signed voltage changes around a closed loop sum to zero. It is energy accounting in the ordinary lumped circuits considered here. Pick a direction around the loop and keep the signs consistent. Going through our source from GND to SUPPLY gives a +3.0 V rise; the resistor drops must total −3.0 V.

With R1 and R2 both 10 kΩ, their series resistance is 20 kΩ. Current is 3.0 / 20,000 = 150 µA. Each resistor drops 150 µA × 10 kΩ = 1.5 V, so MID is 1.5 V relative to GND. If the red meter probe touches MID and black touches GND, expect +1.5 V. Reverse the probes and expect −1.5 V. A negative reading is often useful information about your reference direction, not evidence that the circuit is broken.

The divider expression follows directly from that reasoning:

V_MID = V_SUPPLY × R2 / (R1 + R2)

Notice that R2 is the resistor between the measured node and the reference node. Memorizing “use the bottom resistor” becomes unreliable when a schematic is rotated. Naming the actual nodes survives changes in drawing orientation. MIT's Circuits and Electronics course develops this style of circuit reasoning through lectures and problems.

Power explains why some mistakes become hot

Electrical power is the rate of energy transfer. For a component with a voltage drop V and current I entering its higher-potential terminal, absorbed power is P = VI. The unit is the watt, W. Substituting Ohm's law for a resistor gives P = I²R = V²/R.

Each divider resistor dissipates 1.5 × 0.00015 = 0.000225 W, or 0.225 mW. The source supplies 3.0 × 0.00015 = 0.00045 W. The two resistor powers add to the source power, giving another useful check on the arithmetic. If a calculation said each resistor dissipated 225 W, the unit conversion would deserve immediate attention.

A resistor's wattage rating limits how much heat it may dissipate under specified conditions. Resistance value and power rating are different properties. A 100 Ω resistor across 12 V would dissipate 12² / 100 = 1.44 W; a physically small resistor rated for 0.25 W is unsuitable. Real design also considers temperature, mounting, tolerances, and derating, meaning operating below a maximum rating to accommodate actual conditions.

An LED introduces a nonlinear device. If a particular LED drops approximately 2.0 V at a desired 2 mA from a 3.3 V source, its series resistor needs approximately (3.3 − 2.0) / 0.002 = 650 Ω. Choosing 680 Ω gives about 1.9 mA under that assumption. The resistor dissipates only about 2.5 mW. The LED drop is not an exact universal constant; check its datasheet and calculate worst cases before relying on the current. The resistor must remain in series with the LED, wherever you place the pair in the loop.

Why the divider cannot power the logger

Suppose you connect a third 10 kΩ resistor, RL, from MID to GND. It is in parallel with R2: both ends connect to the same two nodes. Their equivalent resistance is 5 kΩ, because two identical current paths together conduct twice as much current at the same voltage.

The source now sees 10 kΩ in series with 5 kΩ. Source current rises to 3 / 15,000 = 200 µA. MID falls to 200 µA × 5 kΩ = 1.0 V. At MID, 200 µA enters through R1 and splits into 100 µA through each lower resistor. The source still provides 3 V; the circuit's loading has changed the division ratio.

This is why a resistor divider can provide a signal to a suitably high-impedance input but usually cannot replace a power regulator. Input impedance describes how much an input loads a signal; for this DC example it behaves like an input resistance. A meter also draws some current. If its input resistance is very large relative to our divider resistors, its effect is small. If not, the measurement changes the value being measured. Later, ADC inputs add time-dependent loading as well.

Put the meter in the right circuit

A voltmeter is connected across two nodes. Its high input resistance aims to disturb the circuit little. An ammeter measures current by becoming part of the current path, usually with a low-resistance internal shunt. Connecting a meter configured for current directly across a supply can create a short circuit. This lab deliberately obtains current from measured resistor voltage instead.

Before applying power, put the black lead in COM and the red lead in the meter's voltage/resistance socket, according to its manual. Select DC voltage and a suitable range if it is not automatic. Do not leave the red lead in a current socket after a previous task. Resistance and continuity measurements use the meter's own stimulus, so make them only on an unpowered circuit, with stored charge discharged as appropriate. Adafruit's multimeter guide demonstrates the measurement modes; your specific meter manual remains the authority for sockets and limits.

Breadboards add another source of mistaken assumptions. A group of holes may be connected internally, while a neighboring group is separate. Power rails may be split halfway along. A component's two leads placed in one connected group are electrically shorted together. Map these connections with continuity mode while the board is unpowered. A tidy photograph cannot replace a node map.

Electronics · Chapter 12

Signals, sensors, and power: preserve the meaning of a measurement

Builds on11 · Circuits you can explain

The divider from the circuit chapter produces a predictable voltage. A sensor makes that voltage—or a digital message—depend on temperature, position, light, or another physical quantity. The logger's job is to preserve that meaning through wiring, conversion, timing, and storage. A long column of numbers is useful only when you know what each number measures and what can make it wrong.

This chapter follows one signal into a microcontroller and follows the power that makes the measurement possible. You will distinguish resolution from accuracy, calculate a simple filter, recognize aliasing, and make a supply budget. Prerequisites are voltage, current, resistance, and power. The main lab runs on a laptop with no electronic hardware.

Follow the quantity through the chain

Write a measurement chain before selecting parts: physical quantity → sensor → electrical signal → conversion → engineering units. A hypothetical temperature sensor might output 0.50 V at 0°C and increase by 10 mV per degree Celsius. A measured 0.75 V then represents (0.75 − 0.50) / 0.010 = 25°C. Those numbers define this teaching sensor; they are not a specification for every analog temperature sensor.

An analog signal can vary continuously within its range. An analog-to-digital converter, ADC, assigns a numerical code to a sampled input. A digital sensor performs some conversion internally and sends a coded result. It still has an analog sensing element, uncertainty, response time, and a power supply. A digital connector moves some engineering work inside the package; it does not remove it.

Read a candidate sensor's datasheet in this order: operating supply range, absolute maximum limits, interface levels, pinout, measurement range, transfer equation, accuracy conditions, sample or update rate, and recommended circuit. Absolute maximum values are damage boundaries, not normal design targets. A breakout board may add a regulator or level shifting, but only its actual schematic can establish that. An I²C label by itself does not mean a board accepts 5 V signals.

Then inspect conditions underneath the attractive headline. Is the accuracy typical or guaranteed? Does it apply only around room temperature? Does a stated response time include the protective enclosure? Does a measurement consume more current than standby? These questions connect the component to the experiment you intend to perform.

More digits do not guarantee more knowledge

A 12-bit ADC has 2¹² = 4096 possible codes. For an illustrative unipolar input range of 0 to 3.3 V, the ideal code width is 3.3 / 4096 = 0.000806 V, about 0.806 mV. A voltage near half scale produces a code near 2048. This width is a quantization step, not a promise that every reading is within 0.806 mV of truth.

For our hypothetical 10 mV/°C sensor, one ideal code corresponds to about 0.806 / 10 = 0.0806°C. Yet a sensor offset of 10 mV already means a 1°C error. Supply-reference error, ADC offset, gain error, nonlinearities, and noise add other effects. Printing ten decimal places does not undo them. A conversion using code × Vref / 4095 maps endpoint codes to endpoint voltages; using code-bin widths involves 4096. Follow the actual ADC's transfer definition rather than silently mixing conventions.

Calibration compares measurements with a trustworthy reference. One reference point can reveal an offset at that point. Two separated points can estimate offset and scale, assuming a sufficiently linear response between them. Neither proves performance everywhere or removes a drifting reference. Record the reference, conditions, conversion equation, and uncertainty with the calibration result.

TI's ADC Precision Labs videos explain conversion architectures, error sources, and sampling through short lessons. Start with basic operation and aliasing; the advanced input-driver lessons become useful when a circuit meets the voltage limits yet produces unreliable readings.

A capacitor gives the circuit a memory

A capacitor stores charge. Its capacitance is measured in farads, F; a microfarad, µF, is one millionth of a farad. With resistor R from an input node to an output node and capacitor C from the output to GND, the output cannot instantly follow an abrupt input change. Current through R charges C, and the charging slows as their voltage difference shrinks.

For an initially discharged capacitor and an ideal step from 0 to V, the output is V × (1 − e^(−t/RC)). You do not need calculus to use its most helpful landmark: the time constant τ = RC. After one time constant the output reaches about 63.2% of its final value; after five it reaches about 99.3%.

For R = 10 kΩ and C = 1 µF, τ = 0.010 s = 10 ms. A 3.3 V step produces approximately 2.086 V after 10 ms and 3.278 V after 50 ms. The filter smooths rapid changes but introduces delay. Its cutoff frequency is fc = 1 / (2πRC) ≈ 15.9 Hz; at that frequency its sinusoidal output amplitude is about 0.707 times the input, not zero. A single RC filter gradually attenuates higher frequencies rather than cutting them off sharply.

The ADC may complicate this model. Many ADC inputs briefly connect an internal sampling capacitor to the signal. The source must charge it sufficiently during the acquisition interval. A high-resistance divider or an added filter can make that settling too slow. Consult the converter's recommended source impedance and acquisition timing; an amplifier used as a buffer may be needed. “The voltage is within range” and “the input settles accurately” are separate requirements.

Sampling can create a convincing false signal

Sampling frequency, measured in hertz, Hz, is the number of samples per second. Sampling at 20 Hz observes the input every 0.05 seconds. If an unwanted signal changes between observations, its samples may be indistinguishable from a slower signal. That ambiguity is aliasing.

Consider an 18 Hz sine wave sampled at 20 Hz. At those exact sample times, its values equal those of a negative 2 Hz sine wave. A plot of the samples can therefore suggest a slow oscillation that was not present in the physical input. After sampling, no arithmetic can determine which of those indistinguishable inputs occurred without additional information.

For a signal strictly limited below a highest frequency, sampling faster than twice that frequency is the theoretical boundary for reconstruction. Real designs need additional margin and an analog filter before sampling because real filters are not perfect. A motor's switching noise can alias into a slowly varying temperature measurement. A digital moving average applied afterward cannot reliably remove an alias already mixed into the wanted band.

Averaging independent, zero-mean random noise can reduce its standard deviation approximately with the square root of the number of samples: sixteen such samples give about one quarter of the noise. Offset, drift, correlated interference, and repeated copies of the same erroneous reading do not obey that improvement. Compare a quieter display with an independent reference before calling it more accurate.

The power system is part of the measurement

Make a current budget by operating mode. Suppose a processor uses 30 mA, a sensor 10 mA, and an indicator 2 mA from 3.3 V while measuring. The rail needs 42 mA in that mode, or 3.3 × 0.042 = 0.139 W. Startup, radio transmission, storage writes, and actuator motion may create larger short demands. A budget must include both sustained and transient requirements.

A linear regulator dropping 5.0 V to 3.3 V at 42 mA dissipates approximately (5.0 − 3.3) × 0.042 = 0.071 W, ignoring its own small current. Raising the load to 400 mA makes that loss 0.68 W. The unchanged voltage hides a major thermal change. A switching regulator can reduce losses but introduces its own layout, ripple, and component requirements. Use the regulator's actual stability and capacitor guidance.

Local decoupling capacitors help supply rapid changes in current through short paths near a chip. Bulk capacitance supports slower or larger local demands. Neither replaces a suitable supply or cures every layout problem. Wire and trace inductance makes a distant capacitor less effective during rapid changes, so placement matters as well as capacitance. Analog Devices' decoupling tutorial explains the physical reasons; follow each chip's recommended circuit rather than adding arbitrary capacitor values everywhere.

An inductive load stores magnetic energy. When its current is interrupted, that energy needs a controlled path. In a simple one-direction low-side coil circuit, a flyback diode is commonly placed across the coil with its cathode at the positive supply and anode at the switched low side. It normally blocks and then conducts the decaying coil current. A reversible H-bridge needs the driver's intended recirculation arrangement; a diode placed blindly across a reversing motor can short one commanded polarity. Use a documented driver design rather than connecting a coil to a processor pin.

Keep motor current out of thin sensor return paths. If both share a resistive wire, a changing motor current creates a changing voltage along the supposed ground reference. Separate the high-current route while preserving the required common reference for non-isolated logic. This is about current paths, not assigning unrelated ground symbols and assuming they communicate magically.

A sensor logger seems simple: read a sensor, save a number, and respond to a button. The difficulty appears when those actions overlap. A button changes while a reading is in progress. A sensor fails to reply. A file write takes longer than usual. The device restarts with its button already held down. Embedded engineering is largely the work of deciding what those situations mean.

This chapter builds a small, testable behavior model before putting it on a board. You will separate raw inputs from accepted events, schedule work without long pauses, and describe recovery states. You need the circuit and sampling chapters plus basic variables, functions, and conditionals from software and data. The main lab uses ordinary Python on a laptop; an optional complete MicroPython adaptation targets one specific board.

What a microcontroller contains

A microcontroller combines a processor with memory and peripherals on one chip. The processor executes instructions. Flash memory commonly holds the program after power is removed; RAM holds temporary working data. Peripherals perform specialized jobs such as timing, analog conversion, counting pulses, and moving bytes across communication interfaces.

A development board adds practical support: a power regulator, USB connection, accessible pins, and sometimes an indicator or radio. Board names and chip names are not interchangeable. Raspberry Pi's Pico documentation, for example, distinguishes the Pico microcontroller boards from Linux-running Raspberry Pi computers and identifies several board variants. A pin assumption valid for one variant may be wrong for another. Official Pico documentation is the starting point for board identification and pinouts.

A general-purpose input/output pin, GPIO, is configured as an input or output. As an input it observes voltage according to specified logic thresholds. As an output it can source or sink only limited current. It cannot directly power a motor. An unconnected input can float, meaning its voltage has no well-defined logic state. A pull-up resistor gives it a weak connection to a positive logic supply; a switch to ground can then deliberately pull it low.

With that wiring, an open button reads high and a pressed button reads low. Convert this electrical fact into a meaningful variable such as pressed = raw_value == 0 near the input boundary. The rest of the program should reason about a press rather than repeatedly remembering whether zero means active.

One press can contain many electrical transitions

Mechanical contacts can bounce: on closing or opening, they make and break contact several times before settling. Counting every raw edge can turn one deliberate press into several commands. A common solution accepts a changed state only after it has remained unchanged for a chosen interval.

Our example keeps three pieces of information: the accepted state, the candidate state most recently observed, and the time that candidate began. Whenever the raw input changes, it replaces the candidate and restarts the timer. When the candidate has persisted for 20 ms and differs from the accepted state, the program accepts it and emits exactly one event. Twenty milliseconds is a teaching choice to evaluate for a real switch and interaction, not a universal debounce requirement.

Consider a synthetic trace that becomes pressed at 10 ms, released at 12, pressed at 15, released at 17, then pressed continuously from 20 ms onward. The candidate timer restarts at every transition. At 40 ms it has seen twenty uninterrupted milliseconds of the pressed state, so it reports one press. On release, transitions at 100, 103, and 105 ms settle in the released state. The accepted release occurs at 125 ms.

This rule rejects a 19 ms glitch, but it also intentionally ignores a real tap shorter than the required stable duration. Every filter encodes a tradeoff. Before reducing a debounce interval to make an interface feel faster, decide how many extra events you can tolerate and measure the actual switch behavior.

A loop should keep several promises

The simplest firmware repeatedly reads inputs, updates its model, performs any work that is due, and repeats. A half-second sleep inside that loop prevents ordinary polling during the sleep. The display may blink correctly while the program misses a brief input entirely.

Instead, store when each task last ran and compare it with the current clock. Our heartbeat changes the indicator state when at least 500 ms has elapsed, while button processing runs on every pass. A full indicator on/off cycle then takes approximately one second. The short sleep at the end of the board example yields between polls; it does not promise exact one-millisecond scheduling. USB printing, interpreter overhead, and other work can add delay.

Microcontroller tick counters eventually wrap back to zero. Subtracting two raw tick values can then produce an incorrect interval. The supplied hardware program uses MicroPython's ticks_diff for elapsed time, with checks occurring often enough to satisfy its documented half-counter-period limit. It uses the same decision logic as the laptop example while changing how elapsed time is calculated. MicroPython's time API explains this contract.

Periodic sampling introduces another choice. Scheduling the next read relative to the planned deadline preserves a regular intended cadence, but a late program must decide whether to skip missed samples or catch up. Scheduling relative to the actual completion avoids immediate catch-up bursts but drifts. Record actual timestamps rather than pretending every sample occurred exactly on schedule. The right policy depends on whether you are recording slow temperature changes, controlling motion, or capturing a waveform.

Interrupts and watchdogs solve specific problems

Polling observes a pin whenever the program reaches the read operation. An interrupt asks the processor to respond to a hardware event without waiting for that point in the ordinary loop. Interrupts are useful for events that polling might miss, but they introduce concurrency: ordinary code and interrupt code can touch the same information at different moments.

Keep an interrupt handler short. Often it records a timestamp, increments a counter, or places an event in a bounded buffer for ordinary code to process. Printing, waiting, or doing a long sensor transaction inside it can disrupt other deadlines. Shared counters and buffers need deliberate handling so one context cannot observe a half-completed update. A slowly operated button usually does not justify starting with interrupts.

A watchdog is a timer that resets a system if the program fails to report healthy progress within its deadline. Feeding it on every loop regardless of sensor or task health can conceal failures. Feed it only when the checks representing useful progress have succeeded. Record reset causes when available, and make startup outputs safe. Rebooting repeatedly into the same bad command is not recovery. Test watchdog behavior deliberately on a low-energy setup; do not infer it from a blinking indicator.

A breadboard lets you change a circuit quickly. A printed circuit board, PCB, makes its connections repeatable, gives components mechanical support, and can fit the circuit into a product. It also freezes mistakes into copper. The goal of a first PCB is therefore to preserve a circuit you understand while learning how its physical form changes its behavior.

This chapter connects the logger's small circuits to schematic capture, layout, fabrication review, and the semiconductor devices inside integrated circuits. You will distinguish a symbol from a footprint, trace a current's return path, and prepare a simple board for review without needing to order it. Prerequisites are the circuit and signal chapters; basic embedded behavior helps explain the digital examples. The core lab uses free KiCad or graph paper.

Four descriptions of one component

A schematic symbol describes electrical function and pin connections. A footprint describes copper pads, holes, outlines, and assembly clearances on the board. A manufacturer part number identifies an actual component with dimensions and ratings. A 3D model helps inspect shape and fit. These descriptions must agree, but none automatically proves the others correct.

For example, a two-pin connector symbol might connect pin 1 to SUPPLY and pin 2 to GND. If its footprint numbering is reversed relative to the real connector, an electrically tidy schematic can become a reversed power connection. A plausible 3D rendering may still use the wrong pin assignment. Check the manufacturer's drawing, including whether a view looks from the mating face or the solder side.

Record the selected part number, footprint, voltage or power rating, and relevant datasheet in the bill of materials, BOM. A description such as “10 kΩ resistor” is enough for an early circuit calculation but incomplete for assembly: tolerance, package, lead spacing, and power rating still matter. Save custom symbols and footprints with the project so another computer can reproduce it.

A PCB still has a complete circuit

A trace is a shaped copper conductor. A pad provides a soldering or connection surface. A via connects copper between layers through a plated hole. A copper zone fills a region connected to a named net, often ground. The stackup specifies the sequence and thickness of copper and insulating layers. These physical choices affect resistance, capacitance, inductance, heat flow, and manufacturability.

Current that leaves a source must return. For slowly changing DC, resistance strongly shapes how return current divides between available paths. For rapidly changing signals, inductance matters, and a nearby continuous reference plane provides a compact return path. Cutting a slot through that plane beneath a signal can force the return to take a detour, enlarging the loop. A slow repetition rate does not guarantee a slow electrical edge: a digital signal toggling only occasionally may still change voltage very quickly.

Place circuits with their current paths in mind. Keep sensitive sensor connections away from noisy switching loops, and put decoupling connections close to the relevant supply and ground pins. Avoid assuming that splitting a ground plane automatically separates noise; the return still needs to get home. Analog Devices' mixed-signal layout guidance explains placement, layering, and ground-plane tradeoffs. A component's reference layout is often a useful starting constraint, especially for switching power supplies.

Our first resistor-divider board is deliberately forgiving. Its currents are small and its signal changes slowly. That makes it a good place to learn connectivity and assembly, but it does not validate the same layout habits for a radio, motor driver, or fast ADC.

Checks answer different questions

Electrical rules checking, ERC, examines schematic relationships such as incompatible pin types and unconnected pins. Design rules checking, DRC, examines layout constraints such as copper clearance, track width, and unconnected nets. Both depend on the rules and component definitions you supplied. Neither knows that you selected the wrong sensor, reversed a physical connector, or calculated an unsuitable resistor.

Use the intended manufacturer's capabilities to set minimum trace widths, spacings, hole sizes, and copper-to-edge clearance. Minimum manufacturable geometry is not necessarily good engineering geometry: wider traces can reduce resistance, larger clearances can simplify assembly, and accessible test points can save hours later. Voltage, current, temperature, frequency, and assembly method determine what else is needed. There is no universal “safe PCB default” covering every application.

KiCad's getting-started guide walks through schematic capture, footprint assignment, board layout, and fabrication outputs. The interface examples currently document version 10; match the documentation to your installed version. Use the PCB editor manual when you need the exact meaning of a rule or output setting.

What is happening inside the black package?

Semiconductors allow material structure and electric fields to control conduction. Doping changes the available charge carriers in regions of silicon. A junction between differently doped regions produces a diode whose current depends strongly and nonlinearly on applied voltage. It does not behave like a resistor with one fixed resistance. Forward drop, reverse voltage, leakage, current, and temperature all matter.

A transistor adds control. A bipolar transistor uses current into its base as part of controlling collector current. A MOSFET uses the electric field from an insulated gate to control a channel between source and drain. Its gate draws little steady DC current ideally, but switching requires charging and discharging capacitance. Fast switching can therefore require substantial brief drive current.

For an N-channel low-side switch, the source is commonly at GND and the load sits between the positive supply and drain. The relevant control voltage is gate relative to source. A gate pull-down gives an intentional off state while the controller is disconnected or starting. Real designs must also address gate drive, losses, transients, and the load's stored energy.

Do not choose a MOSFET solely because its threshold voltage is below 3.3 V. Threshold identifies the onset of conduction under a specified small-current test; it does not establish a low on-resistance at your load. Find RDS(on) specified at an available gate-source voltage, then check current, temperature, and thermal conditions. Nexperia's MOSFET datasheet guide explains these parameters. For an illustrative 0.050 Ω on-resistance carrying 0.20 A, conduction loss is I²R = 0.002 W; at 2 A it becomes 0.20 W. Switching losses and changing resistance with temperature are additional terms.

Digital circuits assign voltage ranges to logic zero and one. They require margins between a guaranteed output level and the next input's threshold. “Digital” does not mean voltage is irrelevant or that 5 V can safely enter a 3.3 V pin.

15. Software and data: make the measurement explain itself

A sensor logger can produce thousands of numbers without producing one trustworthy measurement. Suppose a distance trace jumps from 20 to 40. Did the part move 20 millimetres, did the sensor restart, or did the program read a voltage as a distance? Software becomes an engineering tool when the recorded evidence lets another person distinguish those explanations.

This chapter takes the bench sensor from the electronics chapters and gives its data a reproducible path from raw counts to reported distance. You will read a small Python program, verify its calibration, reject malformed observations, and preserve a change in version control. You need arithmetic, a text editor, a terminal, and Python 3. No sensor or purchased software is required. If programming is entirely new, allow time to work through variables, loops, and functions in CS50's free Python course alongside this chapter; completing the lab is a starting point rather than a substitute for that practice.

A program is a sequence of changes to state

An instruction runs with some current state: values in memory, an open file, a clock reading, perhaps a motor command. It reads some of that state and changes some of it. A variable is a name you use to refer to a value. An expression calculates a value; an assignment associates it with a name. In distance_mm = (raw_count - 100) * 0.05, the right side is evaluated before distance_mm refers to the result.

A conditional selects a path. A loop repeats a path for several observations. A function groups a transformation with a named interface. Our calibrate_mm(raw) function accepts one count and returns one distance; it does not open files, update a graph, or command a mechanism. That narrow responsibility makes it possible to test the arithmetic without a connected sensor.

Keep a distinction between a value and its representation. The text "500" read from a CSV file is a sequence of characters. Converting it with int() produces the integer 500. The text "five hundred" cannot be converted this way. That failure is an exception: the normal computation stops and control passes to an appropriate handler. Catch expected failures at the boundary where outside data enters; do not silence every exception, because an unrelated programming error would then masquerade as a missing measurement. The Python tutorial provides the language reference path once these ideas are familiar.

State also explains a common debugging surprise. If two names refer to the same mutable list, changing the list through either name changes the one object. Saving an earlier result requires an intentional copy or a new immutable value. Start with simple numbers and short records so that you can trace every change on paper before introducing shared, long-lived objects.

Python on the laptop, C near the pins

Python is convenient for parsing files, drawing conclusions, and experimenting. A typical desktop program runs under an operating system, which allocates memory and schedules it alongside other applications. A requested pause of 10 milliseconds does not promise that the next instruction will run exactly 10 milliseconds later. This matters when a control loop depends on precise timing.

Microcontroller firmware often uses C or C++. The compiled program operates close to registers, buffers, timers, and fixed memory limits. A GPIO peripheral can change a pin because software writes a particular hardware register; a hardware timer can keep time while other instructions execute. This does not automatically make every firmware loop punctual. Blocking serial output, long interrupt handlers, and unbounded work can still delay it.

The numerical rules deserve attention when translating an algorithm. In Python, 3 / 2 produces 1.5, while 3 // 2 produces 1. For positive integer operands in C, 3 / 2 produces the integer 1. Declaring a fractional result later cannot restore a fraction already discarded. Fixed-width integer types also have limited ranges. An eight-bit unsigned count can represent 0 through 255; it cannot hold the number of samples in a long experiment. Signed overflow in C is not a reliable wraparound mechanism.

Choose data types and units together. Use a clearly named elapsed-time value and understand how its clock behaves at rollover. Use a wide enough accumulator when summing samples. Keep raw integer counts until conversion, then use a suitable floating-point representation for engineering values. You do not need to master two languages at once: establish a tested transformation on the laptop, then examine the target language's representation and timing before moving it into firmware.

Work one calibration all the way through

Imagine a deliberately simple linear displacement sensor. A reference at 0 mm produces 100 counts; a reference at 40 mm produces 900 counts. The change is 800 counts over 40 mm, so the scale is:

scale = (40 − 0) mm / (900 − 100) counts = 0.05 mm/count

The zero offset is not zero counts. Subtract it before applying the scale:

distance_mm = (raw_count − 100) × 0.05

At 500 counts, the result is (500 − 100) × 0.05 = 20 mm. At 300 counts, it is 10 mm. A program that instead multiplies the raw count by 0.05 reports 25 mm at the midpoint: a constant 5 mm offset across the range. That recognizable pattern helps identify a missing zero correction.

Two points determine a straight line; they do not demonstrate that the physical sensor is linear. Check additional positions, approaching them from both directions. A repeatable curved error suggests the line is an inadequate model. A difference between forward and reverse travel suggests hysteresis, backlash, or mounting movement. Random scatter needs a repeatability estimate. Store the raw values so that you can apply a better calibration later without pretending to have repeated the physical experiment.

Our synthetic converter accepts the nominal 10-bit range, 0–1023. Counts outside the calibrated interval 100–900 are mathematically extrapolated. That is a separate issue from being outside the converter's possible range. A real instrument should flag extrapolation according to its intended use; silently clipping every reported distance to 0–40 mm would hide evidence of a fault or an out-of-range part.

Debug by narrowing the explanation

When a program fails, start with the smallest input that reproduces the failure. Write the expected result beside the actual one. 16. Feedback control: correct the error without creating a new one

Builds on02 · Math and physics at the workbench10 · Mechanisms, motors and motion12 · Sensors, signals and power15 · Software, data and reproducible work

Giving a motor a command does not specify exactly where a mechanism will end up. Friction, supply voltage, load, and temperature alter the response. Feedback measures what happened and uses the difference from the desired result to choose the next command. This closes a loop through both software and the physical world, so a mistake in either can grow on every pass.

You will predict proportional-control offset, see how integral action removes it, and reproduce a case in which an actuator limit makes an otherwise useful controller behave badly. The lab is a simulation requiring Python 3 and arithmetic; the software/data chapter supplies the programming background. It does not energize a motor. A successful simulated controller is a hypothesis to test on an appropriately guarded, low-energy mechanism, not evidence that the existing pivot is ready for actuation.

Draw the loop before choosing gains

Let r be a reference, y the measured output, and e = r − y the error. A controller converts error into command u. The plant is the mechanism being controlled: motor, load, friction, structure, and relevant electronics. A sensor returns a measurement. The next calculation begins with that measurement, so sensing delay and incorrect signs are part of the loop.

Use units on the drawing. If r and y are position in millimetres and u is a normalized motor command, proportional gain has units of command per millimetre. If you change position from millimetres to metres without changing the numerical gain, the effective response changes by a factor of a thousand. “Gain 2” is meaningless without the signal definitions.

A disturbance changes the plant's behavior without changing your reference: someone adds a load, friction increases, or a fan blows on a heated object. Measurement noise changes what the controller thinks happened. These enter different locations. Raising gain may reject a slow disturbance more strongly while sending more sensor noise to the actuator. There is no single “more accurate” knob independent of the rest of the loop.

Before any physical closed-loop experiment, verify the direction with a tiny permitted command while the mechanism is in a safe state. A positive position command should cause the sensor reading to increase under your convention. If it decreases, the feedback can reinforce the error. Gain tuning cannot repair the wrong sign.

A simple plant makes the arithmetic visible

Our simulated output is dimensionless and tends toward the applied command with a time constant of one second. The update is:

next_y = y + (dt / tau) × (u − y)

Here dt = 0.02 s and tau = 1 s. If y = 0 and u = 0.9, the next output is 0 + 0.02 × 0.9 = 0.018. The change becomes smaller as output approaches command. This is a first-order model: it has one evolving state and no explicit inertia, backlash, or transport delay.

A proportional controller uses u = Kp × e. With Kp = 2, reference 0.6, and initial output 0, it requests 1.2. Our actuator can apply only values from 0 to 1, so it applies 1. The first output is 0.02. The second request is 2 × (0.6 − 0.02) = 1.16, still limited to 1. Requested command and applied command are different signals; log both.

At equilibrium, this model has y = u. If the command is no longer limited, then y = Kp(r − y). Rearranging gives y = Kp × r / (1 + Kp). For Kp = 2 and r = 0.6, the settled output is 0.4 and the remaining error is 0.2. The controller needs some error to maintain a nonzero command. This explains the offset without assuming that the code is broken.

This result belongs to this plant and this controller. A position plant containing an integrator behaves differently. Do not carry the formula to every motor and conclude that all proportional controllers must have this exact offset.

Integral action remembers persistent error

An integrator accumulates error over time. In our implementation the integral contribution is already in command units:

next_integral = integral + Ki × e × dt

The PI request is Kp × e + integral. A small error that persists keeps increasing the contribution until the plant receives enough command to reach the reference. With zero steady error, the integrator can retain the command needed to hold that output. For a position error in millimetres, Ki would have units of command per millimetre per second.

Time is essential. Omitting dt means that changing the loop from 50 to 100 updates per second doubles the accumulation rate. A gain that seemed successful then changes merely because the program runs faster. Use measured elapsed time where appropriate, and establish a policy for a missed deadline rather than allowing a very late update to produce a huge unexpected increment.

Integral action has a cost: stored history continues to act after the reference changes. If a command is limited, the integrator may keep accumulating a demand the actuator cannot deliver. This is windup. A motor already at maximum drive cannot move faster just because the software requests ten times the maximum. After the target comes down, the stored integral can keep the actuator at maximum while the current error asks it to slow down.

Our anti-windup rule conditionally stops accumulation when the proposed command is outside its range and the error would push farther into that limit. It allows integration that moves the command back toward the usable range. Other designs feed the difference between requested and applied commands back into the integrator. The MathWorks PID video series provides separate visual treatments of saturation, anti-windup, and derivative noise; its software demonstrations are optional for this lab.

Why more gain can make the response worse

For the unsaturated proportional case, substituting the controller into the update leaves a coefficient of 1 − dt(1 + Kp)/tau multiplying the previous output. A disturbance shrinks on each iteration only if the absolute value of that coefficient is less than one. This simple numerical model therefore requires 0 < dt(1 + Kp)/tau < 2 for that behavior.

With tau = 1, Kp = 2, and dt = 0.02, the coefficient is 0.94: successive deviations decay without changing sign. With dt = 0.8, it is −1.4: deviations alternate and grow. Saturation may bound the displayed output, but a bounded oscillation is not successful regulation. This is a stability condition for this particular discrete simulation, not a universal gain limit.

The real plant introduces additional reasons to lose stability. A delayed measurement describes an earlier position. Flexible parts can continue moving after the motor changes direction. Backlash creates motion that an input-side encoder cannot see. Large gains can excite these effects. Begin with a model that exposes the essential response, then add measured complications when the simpler model cannot explain the trace.

Derivative action responds to the rate at which a signal changes. A small noisy difference divided by a short interval can become a large derivative. Filtering can reduce that noise but adds delay. A moving average over many samples is therefore a control decision, not merely a cosmetic improvement to a graph. This chapter uses PI so that the roles of stored error and actuator limits remain visible before adding derivative filtering and more tuning choices.

A sensible bridge from simulation to a rig

Define startup, enabled, stopping, and fault states before writing a live loop. Decide what happens when feedback is stale, a limit is reached, the computer disconnects, or the sensor reports an impossible jump. A user-interface stop button is not a substitute for an appropriately designed physical means to remove hazardous energy. The right stop response depends on the mechanism: removing drive from a supported horizontal rig and releasing a gravity-loaded arm are different events.

For a future low-energy bench rig, establish sensor scale and direction with actuation disabled, measure a small open-loop response within known travel, and compare it to the model. Then use conservative output and travel limits, a reachable target, and logging of actual timestamps. Look at friction, delay, and repeatability before increasing gains. Keep the early experiments supported and unloaded; the mechanical load test and electrical checks remain separate prerequisites.

If the output is noisy only while the actuator runs, investigate electrical interference, grounding, vibration, and supply behavior before trying to tune it away. If it drifts slowly despite PI, inspect saturation and sensor bias. If the trace shows regular pauses, inspect scheduling and blocking work. These explanations lead to different repairs even when all three symptoms are described as “unstable.”

17. Robotics: agree on where things are before moving them

Builds on10 · Mechanisms, motors and motion13 · Microcontrollers and embedded behavior15 · Software, data and reproducible work16 · Feedback and control

A robot combines a mechanism, sensors, computation, and actuation. Each part can work on its own while the assembled system behaves incorrectly. A camera reports a point relative to its lens; a motion planner assumes a point relative to the robot base; a motor driver expects counts relative to an encoder zero. If those meanings are not connected explicitly, a perfectly valid number can send the mechanism in the wrong direction.

This chapter builds a small planar robot model and uses it to connect coordinate frames, kinematics, sensing, and integration. You will calculate a tool position, find two joint configurations that reach it, and identify a target the mechanism cannot reach. Prerequisites are the measurement, software, and feedback chapters, plus basic sine and cosine. The lab needs Python 3 or paper, a ruler, and a protractor. Motors and ROS are optional later extensions; no actuated pivot is assumed to have been validated.

Give every position a frame and a time

A coordinate frame is an origin plus directions for its axes. “The object is at x = 20 mm” is incomplete unless you know where x starts and which way it points. Put a base frame on the tabletop and draw positive x to the right and positive y away from you. Place another frame on the camera. Its axes may be rotated relative to the tabletop, and its origin is generally elsewhere.

A two-dimensional rotation by angle a maps a local point (x, y) into (x cos a − y sin a, x sin a + y cos a). Add the location of the local origin to express the point in the destination frame. The order matters: rotating a local point and then translating it describes a different operation from translating it and then rotating the entire result.

Suppose the camera's local x-axis points along the base's positive y-axis: a 90° rotation. Its origin is at (50, 30) mm in the base frame. A camera-frame point (20, 0) mm rotates to (0, 20) mm, then translates to (50, 50) mm. Adding the camera offset without rotating would incorrectly give (70, 30) mm.

A position also belongs to a time. At 100 mm/s, a 0.10-second-old observation can be 10 mm behind the current position, even if every coordinate conversion is correct. Record the measurement time, not merely the time the application finishes processing it. A moving camera needs the camera-to-base relationship at that measurement time. This is why robotics messages often carry frame identifiers and timestamps together.

Kinematics describes motion geometry

Imagine two rigid links connected by revolute joints in a plane. The first link is 100 mm long and the second is 80 mm. Joint angle q1 is the first link's angle relative to base x; q2 is the second link's angle relative to the first link. Positive angles turn counterclockwise. The second link's angle relative to the base is therefore q1 + q2.

The endpoint coordinates follow by adding the two link vectors:

x = 100 cos(q1) + 80 cos(q1 + q2)

y = 100 sin(q1) + 80 sin(q1 + q2)

For q1 = 30° and q2 = −60°, the second link points at −30° relative to the base. Thus x = 100 × 0.8660 + 80 × 0.8660 = 155.885 mm, while y = 100 × 0.5 + 80 × (−0.5) = 10 mm. This is forward kinematics: joint configuration in, tool position out.

The calculation assumes rigid links and exact joint locations. A long printed link may deflect under load; an encoder may measure a motor before a flexible transmission. Kinematics describes the nominal geometry. Dynamics concerns the forces and torques required to produce motion, and structural analysis concerns deformation and failure. A reachable point is not evidence that a loaded mechanism can hold it.

For orientation and homogeneous-transform foundations, the Modern Robotics companion lessons provide a deeper progression. Start with configuration and rigid-body motion before inverse kinematics; the vocabulary becomes much easier once you have drawn a frame yourself.

Inverse kinematics has choices and boundaries

Inverse kinematics asks which joint angles reach a desired point. For our two-link arm, the distance from the base gives:

cos(q2) = (x² + y² − 100² − 80²) / (2 × 100 × 80)

The cosine of a real angle must lie between −1 and 1. A target at (190, 0) mm would require a cosine greater than 1, so it is impossible even before considering obstacles. The arm's maximum reach is 180 mm; its minimum radius is 20 mm because the longer link cannot be completely cancelled by the shorter one.

At most ordinary reachable positions there are two solutions: one with the elbow on one side of the base-to-tool line and one on the other. Our worked point has solutions approximately (30°, −60°) and (−22.659°, 60°). They share an endpoint but occupy different space. A table edge, cable, joint stop, or nearby object may allow only one.

Near a fully straight or folded configuration, small Cartesian changes can require disproportionately large joint changes in certain directions. This is a singularity: the local mapping between joint motion and tool motion loses a direction. Do not infer a safe trajectory from a list of individually reachable endpoints. The path between them must also respect joint limits, collision clearance, speed, acceleration, and the chosen elbow branch.

Sensors and actuators report different kinds of truth

An incremental encoder reports changes in rotation; establishing absolute position usually requires a known reference or additional sensing. An absolute encoder reports a position code within its specified range, but its mounting zero, gear relationship, and multi-turn behavior still matter. A limit switch indicates a boundary or reference event; it does not measure the entire motion.

A stepper driver can issue a known number of steps while the motor loses motion under load. Counting commands then estimates intended position rather than measuring actual position. A hobby servo usually closes an internal position loop, but its command interface does not necessarily expose accurate feedback to your application. A geared DC motor with an encoder gives useful measurements, yet gearbox backlash can let the output move without an equivalent motor-side change.

Choose sensing according to the error you must detect. If the experiment concerns tool deflection, an upstream encoder cannot observe all of it. A camera may observe the tool but add latency and lighting sensitivity. Comparing sensors can reveal disagreement, but two sensors sharing the same loose bracket can share the same error.

Actuator selection also needs more than a torque headline. Required speed, continuous heating, supply current, duty cycle, backlash, and holding behavior determine whether the motor fits the task. A simulation can estimate trajectories and nominal loads. Begin physical validation with separate measurements of the mechanism and actuator, then integrate them gradually.

Integration is a sequence of contracts

Break the system into parts with explicit interfaces: sensor acquisition, state estimation, planning, low-level control, and user commands. Define units, frames, timestamps, valid ranges, expected update rates, and failure behavior at each boundary. Replay a recorded sensor trace into the computation before connecting live actuation. Replace one simulated component at a time so that a new discrepancy has a small set of explanations.

A simulator can check geometry, collision models, and software interactions. Its friction, contact stiffness, sensor noise, and delay are assumptions unless identified from measurements. A robot that succeeds only in simulation may be exploiting unrealistic contact, unlimited torque, or perfect sensing. Keep a table of model assumptions and the bench observation that could invalidate each one.

Make operation states explicit. DISABLED permits inspection; READY means prerequisites are satisfied; RUNNING accepts permitted motion; FAULT prevents automatic resumption after a detected problem. A stop request should enter a defined stopping process, and a fault should require an intentional recovery decision. Whether the appropriate physical response is controlled deceleration, drive disable, braking, or another action depends on stored energy and the mechanism. A software state name alone cannot make a gravity-loaded arm safe.

A framework does not determine your units, make a motor controller safe, or compensate for an inaccurate mechanical model.

18. AI and computer vision: build the test before trusting the prediction

Builds on03 · Measure, experiment and debug15 · Software, data and reproducible work

A camera could help a bench rig find a fiducial, check whether a printed part is present, or record how far a loaded bracket bends. “Use AI” is not yet a specification. The useful question is what observation the system must make, under which conditions, with which consequences when it is wrong. A simple light threshold may solve the task more transparently than a trained network; a trained network may handle variation that a threshold cannot.

This chapter develops a small visual classifier and deliberately exposes its weakness under changed lighting. You will separate training from evaluation, calculate a confusion matrix, and define a sensible boundary between an AI assistant and a physical controller. You need the software/data chapter and basic fractions. The lab uses synthetic grayscale images and Python's standard library, so a camera, GPU, account, and machine-learning installation are unnecessary.

Start with an observable decision

Suppose a printed coupon should carry a dark registration mark. The task is to classify a small image region as “mark present” or “mark absent.” A useful requirement might specify the camera distance, lighting range, allowed part rotation, minimum mark size, and acceptable false-accept and false-reject rates. It should also define what happens when the image is blurred or the part is outside the expected region.

The label is the answer assigned to an example, preferably from an independent inspection. The feature is a measured property used to make the decision: number of dark pixels, edge position, or a learned representation. The model maps features to a prediction. Training chooses the model's parameters from examples. Inference applies those fixed parameters to a new example.

Our baseline counts pixels darker than intensity 100 and predicts a mark when the count reaches a learned threshold. It is intentionally limited. It ignores whether the dark pixels form the right shape or occupy the right location. Naming those assumptions makes its failures useful. A complicated model without an explicit baseline can look impressive while offering no measurable improvement over a few understandable operations.

The baseline can also reveal that the physical setup needs improvement. A matte background, fixed camera mount, controlled illumination, and a repeatable part location may remove more variation than a larger model. Engineering the observation is part of computer vision.

Separate learning from the final examination

A model evaluated on its training examples answers “Can this rule fit information it has already seen?” Deployment asks whether it works on a new part, session, or lighting condition. Keep a training set for choosing parameters, a validation set for choosing among approaches, and an untouched test set for the final estimate. With a tiny teaching dataset we use only training and held-out test records, and explicitly avoid tuning on the test results.

Data leakage occurs when information from the evaluation set influences training or model selection. It can be subtle. Adjacent video frames share almost the same object and background. Randomly dividing those frames places near-duplicates on both sides of the split. A high score can then measure recognition of a recording session instead of generalization to new parts.

Split according to the intended deployment uncertainty. If the system must work on newly printed parts, keep all images of one physical part in one group. If lighting changes between sessions, hold out entire sessions. Fit normalization parameters only on training data. Keep the final test labels out of the model-building loop, and record when a supposedly held-out dataset has become familiar enough that it is now effectively validation data.

Statistical learning provides the language for these distinctions. The authors' site for An Introduction to Statistical Learning offers the Python edition and chapter labs; its model assessment material is a useful next step after this deliberately small experiment.

Accuracy can hide the failure you care about

Imagine inspecting 100 parts, of which 95 are acceptable and 5 have defects. A classifier that always says “acceptable” has 95% accuracy while detecting none of the defects. The number sounds strong because the common class dominates it.

Choose the positive class explicitly. In our lab, positive means “mark present.” A true positive is a present mark predicted present. A false positive is an absent mark predicted present. A false negative is a present mark missed. A true negative is a correctly rejected absent mark.

For eight held-out images, our classifier produces 3 true positives, 2 false positives, 2 true negatives, and 1 false negative. Therefore:

accuracy = (3 + 2) / 8 = 0.625

precision = 3 / (3 + 2) = 0.600

recall = 3 / (3 + 1) = 0.750

Precision asks how often a positive prediction is correct. Recall asks how many actual positives are found. Which matters more depends on the process. If a falsely accepted part causes an expensive assembly failure, false positives deserve special attention. If missing a valid mark merely asks an operator to inspect the image, that cost is different. State the decision cost rather than declaring one metric universally best.

Eight synthetic examples cannot estimate a production error rate with useful confidence. The counts teach the arithmetic and reveal failure mechanisms. Real evaluation needs representative independent examples, documented labels, and uncertainty appropriate to the sample size. A confidence score from a model is also not automatically a calibrated probability of correctness.

python3 vision_baseline.py --images vision-images

The second run writes eight plain-text PGM image files. Each contains an 8-by-8 grayscale array with values from 0 to 255; even without an image viewer, a text editor exposes every pixel.

Several count thresholds classify all training records correctly. The code deliberately selects the smallest equally accurate threshold, 3. This tie-breaking rule is part of the experiment and makes the result reproducible.

The test set contains a true mark with only two dark pixels, causing the false negative. It also contains two images from a dim session with background intensity 80. Every pixel in those images now falls below the fixed darkness cutoff of 100. The classifier sees many dark pixels and reports a mark even when none is present. Those are the two false positives.

All images are synthetic fixtures created to isolate a mechanism; they are not photographs of successfully inspected printed parts.

Before changing the algorithm, write a prediction. Would a lower intensity cutoff distinguish a 60-valued mark from an 80-valued background? In this synthetic dataset, yes. Would that guarantee performance on real images? No: shadows, reflections, camera gain, blur, and material color could overlap those intensities. Once you use these test images to choose a new cutoff, they are no longer an untouched final test. Create a new held-out session to evaluate the revised hypothesis.

From pixel decisions to geometric measurements

Finding a dark region and measuring a physical distance are different tasks. A pixel coordinate has no inherent millimetre scale. For a flat target at a fixed distance, a reference object in the same plane can provide a local scale. Move the target closer to the camera and the same physical length spans more pixels. Tilt it and foreshortening changes the apparent shape.

Lens distortion can bend straight edges, especially toward the image boundary. Camera calibration estimates the imaging model from known geometry; camera pose relates that model to the object or robot frame. The OpenCV calibration tutorial explains these separate parameters and a pattern-based workflow. A calibration is tied to the optical configuration; changing focus, image crop, or resolution requires checking whether the previous parameters remain valid.

For a first bench measurement, fix the camera, use a flat target, keep the reference scale in the measurement plane, and collect images at several known distances or positions. Compare the estimated distances against independent measurements. If the error grows near the edges, inspect distortion and perspective. If repeated measurements vary at one position, inspect lighting, blur, feature localization, and mechanical movement. Report the uncertainty rather than rounding a noisy estimate to many decimal places.

A useful model must also fit the device

A laptop demonstration does not establish that a microcontroller or small single-board computer can run the same pipeline. Account for image capture, resizing, model inference, communication, and decision logic. If capture takes 20 ms, preprocessing 5 ms, inference 35 ms, and decision handling 5 ms sequentially, the end-to-end delay is 65 ms. That is more relevant to a moving target than quoting inference time alone.

Memory includes model parameters, intermediate activations, input buffers, and the rest of the application. Power and heat can reduce sustained performance. Quantization can reduce storage and computation but may alter the error distribution; re-evaluate the deployed representation. Measure worst-case or suitably high-percentile latency on the actual device, not only average speed on a development computer.

If you need learned visual features after establishing the baseline, fast.ai's Practical Deep Learning for Coders offers an applied route for people who already write code. For a wider algorithmic foundation, CS50 AI covers search, uncertainty, learning, and language; its stated prerequisite is CS50x or at least one year of Python experience. Neither is the first programming lesson, and external notebooks may require environment adjustments as dependencies change.

Use language models as reviewable assistants

A language model can help explain an unfamiliar function, suggest tests, summarize a log, or draft an implementation. Its fluent answer is not a measurement. Ask for assumptions, units, input/output examples, and a way to falsify the proposed result. Run generated code against known fixtures and inspect the operations that touch files, credentials, networks, or devices.

For physical projects, keep an LLM outside the direct actuator-control path. Let it propose a plan or bounded configuration for human review; deterministic software should enforce ranges, timing, interlocks, and stop behavior. Sensor text, web content, and retrieved documents can contain misleading instructions, so treat them as data rather than authority to alter a machine's permissions.

An assistant that proposes moving to (x, y) still needs a valid frame, reachable geometry, collision checks, a permitted state, and a tested motion controller. A natural-language assurance cannot replace any of those. Use the same discipline as for another contributor's code: review the change, reproduce the result, and retain the evidence.

Check the claim before extending it

Predict: Why can 100% training accuracy coexist with poor deployment? Answer: The model can fit the available examples while relying on a condition, such as lighting, that changes later. Training performance does not test independence or coverage.

Predict: Is a random frame split adequate for a camera recording one part? Answer: Usually not for estimating performance on new parts; near-duplicate frames leak shared information across the split.

Predict: A vision model reports a point with 0.99 confidence. May the robot move there immediately? Answer: No. The point still needs a timestamp, frame transformation, uncertainty and workspace checks, and authorization through the deterministic motion system.

For an independent project, photograph five distinct paper fiducials over several sessions using a fixed camera. Keep one entire session untouched. Compare a threshold baseline with a shape-based rule, documenting lighting, labels, false positives, and false negatives. Stop at an on-screen inspection result. Your deliverable is a reproducible evaluation and failure gallery; connecting the result to motion is a separate integration project with additional checks.

Intelligent systems · Chapter 19

19. Connected devices: make communication failure an ordinary condition

Builds on12 · Sensors, signals and power13 · Microcontrollers and embedded behavior15 · Software, data and reproducible work

A bench logger becomes more useful when its measurements reach a laptop. A positioning rig becomes more complicated when commands arrive over a network. Messages can be late, repeated, lost, malformed, or sent by the wrong device. Designing only for the successful exchange leaves the most consequential behavior unspecified.

This chapter connects the sensor/logger to a larger system while keeping the behavior understandable. You will distinguish board-level buses from network protocols, calculate a realistic serial data budget, and replay duplicate and stale commands through a small receiver. The lab requires the software/data chapter and Python 3; it opens no network connection and controls no hardware. Familiarity with digital voltage levels helps with the optional physical interfaces.

“Serial” describes an arrangement, not one universal connector

Serial communication sends bits over time rather than placing every bit on a separate wire. UART, I²C, and SPI are all serial interfaces, but they differ in timing, addressing, wiring, and electrical assumptions. A USB connector does not imply that the signals on its pins are ordinary microcontroller UART signals. A USB-to-UART adapter translates between different interfaces.

For UART, both ends must agree closely enough on timing for the selected devices and frame length. A peripheral data sheet specifies the tolerances; a label such as “serial” does not. Microchip's UART overview is an entry point to device-specific documentation.

Typical I²C lines are pulled high and devices actively pull them low, allowing a shared bus. Pull-up resistance and capacitance affect how quickly a released line rises. Adding boards and long wires can therefore break timing even when the addresses are correct. NXP's I²C specification documents the electrical and protocol requirements. SPI uses different signaling and selection conventions; its device data sheet remains decisive for the supported mode and timing. See the Microchip SPI overview for the basic signal roles.

Before connecting boards, verify their logic voltage limits and reference arrangement with power removed. A 5 V output may damage a 3.3 V-only input. Do not infer compatibility from a connector fitting. Bare logic-level buses also are not automatically suitable for long external cables; interfaces such as RS-485 use additional electrical transceivers for different wiring conditions.

Work out whether the bytes can fit

Suppose a UART link runs at 115,200 bits per second using eight data bits, no parity, and one stop bit, often written 8N1. A usual frame also has one start bit, so each data byte occupies ten transmitted bits. The theoretical data capacity is therefore 115,200 / 10 = 11,520 bytes/s, before application pauses and other overhead.

If each logger record is 80 bytes including its line ending and the logger produces 50 records per second, the stream needs 80 × 50 = 4,000 bytes/s, about 35% of that capacity. If an expanded JSON record reaches 180 bytes at 100 records per second, it needs 18,000 bytes/s and cannot fit. A growing output buffer may initially hide the mismatch, then create long delays or lost data.

Measure complete encoded records, including timestamps, field names, separators, and line endings. Leave room for bursts, diagnostic messages, and device processing. If logging blocks a time-sensitive control loop, separate acquisition from transmission with a bounded buffer and a documented overflow policy. Dropping an old telemetry sample with a gap counter can be preferable to silently delaying the controller; the correct policy depends on the experiment.

Separate the transport from the meaning

A transport delivers bytes. An application protocol defines what those bytes mean. A useful telemetry record includes a schema version, device identifier, boot/session identifier, sequence number, measurement time, unit, value, and quality status. Not every project needs every field in every packet, but every ambiguity needs a resolution somewhere in the interface contract.

For example, distance_mm: 20 is clearer than value: 20, while raw_count: 500 preserves information needed for future calibration. A sequence number reveals gaps and duplicates within a session. A boot identifier distinguishes a legitimate sequence restart from an old packet arriving late. A timestamp supports age checks only when the receiver understands the clock and its uncertainty.

Frame the stream so that a receiver can recover after a damaged record. Newline-delimited JSON is convenient for small human-readable logs: read a bounded line, parse one record, validate its schema and ranges, then accept or reject it. Limit message size and parsing work. A syntactically valid document can still contain an impossible value, unsupported version, or unauthorized request.

Distinguish a received command from an applied command. A transport acknowledgment confirms a protocol event, not that a mechanism reached its target. The device should separately report accepted, rejected, executing, and completed states as appropriate, with the command identifier attached. This prevents a user interface from displaying success merely because a packet was sent.

HTTP and MQTT organize communication differently

HTTP is a request-and-response application protocol. It suits reading a device status resource, downloading a log, or submitting a bounded configuration change. Use method semantics intentionally: reading a status should not secretly start motion. RFC 9110 defines the common HTTP semantics, including safe and idempotent methods; an endpoint's implementation still has to honor those meanings.

MQTT uses a broker to route published messages to interested subscribers. A logger can publish measurements while a dashboard and archive subscribe independently. Topics provide routing, not a complete data schema or authorization policy. The OASIS MQTT 5 specification defines delivery levels: QoS 0 can lose messages, QoS 1 permits duplicates, and QoS 2 provides an exactly-once protocol delivery mechanism. None alone proves that a physical action completed exactly once across application crashes and restarts.

A repeated “toggle” command reverses the result again. A repeated “set indicator off” command has the same intended state, making it idempotent. Desired-state commands are often easier to recover than incremental instructions, although motion still needs explicit limits, identity, and state checks. Retained messages require particular care: a new subscriber may receive an old stored value. That is useful for a configuration snapshot and potentially inappropriate for an unqualified motion command.

Keep the first network extension local and observable. Eclipse Mosquitto's documentation provides broker, client, authentication, and configuration references. Before allowing access beyond the development machine, configure authentication and permissions deliberately; do not assume a working demonstration has appropriate network exposure.

Replay failure cases without a network

Download message_lab.py and run:

python3 message_lab.py

The receiver begins in an off state. A fresh command with sequence 1 turns the simulated state on. Repeating sequence 1 is rejected as duplicate; sequence 0 is rejected as old. A command sent at time 0 but delivered at time 3 is rejected as expired. By then the two-second timeout has already put the state off. A fresh explicit off command is accepted, and the final timeout leaves it off.

The lab's rule accepts only supported schema version 1, nonnegative integer sequences, states on or off, and timestamps no more than one second old. Accepted commands refresh a local watchdog time; rejected duplicates do not. This prevents a repeated old packet from making the receiver believe that the sender is still providing fresh instructions.

Read tick() separately from receive(). A timeout must run even when no messages arrive. A design that checks for silence only inside a message handler cannot notice total silence. In a real device, use an appropriate monotonic local clock for elapsed watchdog time. The lab uses one simulated clock for sender and receiver; it does not solve clock synchronization or authentication.

Run the shared test suite using the example instructions. Its checks include duplicate handling, expiry, out-of-order delivery, and the fact that a duplicate cannot refresh the watchdog. These are verified software policies for an in-memory example. The selected timeout is a teaching value, not a derived safe stopping interval for a mechanism.

Change the timeout to 0.5 seconds and trace the same events on paper before running them. Then add an event with a new sequence but an old timestamp. A sequence number alone does not establish freshness. Finally, consider a reboot: the in-memory sequence history disappears. A deployable policy must address sessions, persistence, and whether old commands can be replayed after a restart.

Security is part of the device's behavior

Authentication answers which identity is connecting; authorization answers what that identity may do. A dashboard that can read temperature does not necessarily need permission to change calibration or command an actuator. Give devices distinct credentials, limit topic or endpoint permissions, and keep secrets out of public repositories and logs. Encrypt network traffic where required and verify the server identity; encryption without identity checking can protect a conversation with the wrong endpoint.

Plan how a device receives trusted software updates, reports its version, and recovers from an interrupted update. A checksum detects accidental corruption but does not establish who authorized an image. A signed update needs a protected trust decision and a recovery design. Keep a known working recovery route and test it on the development device before relying on remote maintenance.

NISTIR 8259A is a useful requirements lens: device identification, configuration, data protection, interface access, software updates, and awareness of security state. Reading it does not certify a hobby build. Use it to turn vague intentions into questions you can answer about your specific device and its expected environment.

Security and functional limits complement each other. An authenticated command can still be mistaken or unsafe. Keep physical operating limits, stale-data handling, and stop behavior inside the local deterministic system. Loss of a cloud dashboard should not remove the device's ability to enforce its own constraints.

Keep the experiment useful when disconnected

A local-first logger records essential data locally and treats upload as a separate task. Use a bounded queue and decide what happens when storage fills. Preserve sequence gaps and status flags rather than making a continuous-looking graph from incomplete data. When connectivity returns, upload with record identities so the server can recognize duplicates.

Separate “device online” from “measurement fresh.” A connected device may be publishing an old cached value. Show the measurement age, quality, and device state alongside the number. Logs should explain rejected commands, restarts, configuration changes, and dropped records without revealing credentials. A bounded, rotating log prevents diagnosis itself from exhausting the device's storage.

When a link fails, diagnose in layers: power and voltage, signal wiring and timing, byte framing, message schema, authentication, then application state. Garbled UART text suggests different causes from well-formed messages rejected for an expired timestamp. A known small message and a loopback or vendor-supported interface test often isolate the layer faster than rewriting the application.

Check, then design the next connection

Predict: Why might QoS 1 break a toggle command? Answer: Duplicate delivery can toggle twice. A command identifier and idempotent desired-state behavior make retries easier to handle.

Predict: Does a successful network reply prove the mechanism reached its destination? Answer: No. Receipt, acceptance, execution, and verified completion are separate events.

Predict: Why does the receiver need a timeout routine independent of incoming messages? Answer: Total silence produces no message callback, so only a separately scheduled check can detect it.

For an independent project, extend the simulated logger with a bounded offline queue and an upload function that deliberately fails on selected attempts. Give each record a device/session/sequence identity. Demonstrate recovery without duplicate archive entries, document which records are dropped if the queue fills, and show the user how old the newest measurement is. Complete that behavior before adding a real broker or remote actuator; the network then becomes a transport for a policy you can already explain and test.

Practice · Chapter 20

From guided build to independent project

Builds on01 · Work like an inventor03 · Measure, experiment and debug04 · Design for people and purpose

A tutorial gives you a destination and many of the decisions that lead there. An independent project asks you to choose the destination, discover the missing information and decide when the result is adequate. The transition is manageable if you preserve the habits you have practiced: define the job, expose interfaces, predict behavior, test one uncertainty and retain the evidence.

This chapter turns the handbook into three project briefs and a repeatable delivery process. Start with project framing, measurement and product design, then follow the technical prerequisites for your chosen branch. You do not need to finish every chapter before starting a modest project. You do need to know which gaps matter before increasing complexity or energy.

Choose a project with an observable finish

“Build a robot” can expand indefinitely. “Make a tabletop indicator move between two marked positions in simulation, then evaluate a low-energy physical version” has a first finish line. A useful early project combines one familiar skill with one or two new skills, while keeping the consequences of failure manageable.

Write a one-sentence job and three acceptance checks. Each check should name the configuration, action and observable result. Separate required behavior from optional extensions. An enclosure, wireless link and AI classifier can each become another project; adding them all before the basic measurement works makes diagnosis much harder.

A project is allowed to stop with a negative finding. Discovering that a chosen sensor cannot resolve the intended change is a successful feasibility experiment, even if it ends that concept. Record the evidence and the conditions so another person does not need to repeat the same uncertainty blindly.

Define interfaces before detailed implementation

An interface contract records what two subsystems expect from each other. For a mechanical interface, include reference surfaces, dimensions, tolerances, insertion direction and fastener access. For an electrical interface, include voltage range, current demand, signal reference, connector pinout and behavior when power is absent. For software, include field names, units, timing, valid ranges and failure behavior.

Use the interface worksheet. Write the assumptions on both sides. A sensor can be mechanically compatible and electrically incompatible; a message can parse successfully and contain a value in the wrong unit. These are integration problems that isolated component demonstrations will miss.

For example, agree that a logger records distance_mm, an explicit timestamp and a validity flag. If the device cannot produce a valid distance, it should report invalid status rather than silently repeat an old value. The consumer must then decide what invalid data means. In a display it may show a gap; in an actuator controller it may require stopping. The contract joins the data format to behavior.

Test the boundaries as well as the boxes. Many failures occur when individually working parts disagree about units, timing or physical access.

Capstone A: a measured sensor bracket

Result: a removable bracket for a light, noncritical sensor or dimensionally representative dummy, with a drawing, fit coupon, assembly record and placement measurements. Study CAD, materials, printing and mechanics first: Chapters 5–9 provide the relevant concepts. Use the existing pivot tutorial as a detailed CAD and assembly case study when you want a more demanding mechanism.

Materials: paper or card, a ruler or caliper, the actual sensor envelope and connector, suitable ordinary fasteners, access to CAD and a printer or a makerspace. A card-only first stage is valid. Do not purchase the whole bill of materials before measuring the inherited interfaces.

Stage 1 — understand the fit. Measure mounting geometry and connector clearance. Make a full-size card envelope. Record which dimensions are measured and which come from a drawing. Choose reference surfaces and an installation sequence. Stop if the connector or tool cannot reach its working position.

Stage 2 — manufacture the uncertainty. Model a small coupon containing the fit that most affects success: a hole, nut pocket, slide or insert location. Print it using the planned material and orientation. Compare several justified clearance values and record actual assembly behavior. A fit established in a different orientation or material may not transfer unchanged.

Stage 3 — build the minimum complete bracket. Preserve the selected interface dimensions, add only the structure needed for the chosen low-load demonstration and inspect the sliced toolpaths. Record printer profile, orientation, material and revision. Assemble without forcing a mismatched interface. Keep the first failed coupon as evidence if useful.

Acceptance checks: confirm connector and tool access; remove and replace the dummy at least five times using the same reference; compare measured placement spread with your provisional requirement. If a low-load stiffness observation is needed, define the load, fixture, reference and stop condition before testing. Use a harmless supported setup, not an overhead or safety-critical load. This project does not establish a general load rating.

Independent extension: change one inherited dimension, such as sensor width, and regenerate the model. Explain which features should update and which should remain fixed. Then verify the changed interface physically. This tests design intent more deeply than reproducing a shape once.

Capstone B: a low-voltage sensor/logger

Result: a small sensing system that produces interpretable records, handles invalid input and can be restarted without losing the meaning of its data. Study circuits, signals, software and embedded behavior before the hardware integration: Chapters 11–15 supply the pieces.

Materials: begin with the supplied desktop Python labs and synthetic data. For the optional physical stage, choose a documented low-voltage development board and compatible sensor from their official specifications, a suitable protected supply, a breadboard where appropriate and a meter. The chapter-specific circuit defines the connections; this capstone is an integration brief, not a universal wiring diagram. Verify pin voltage limits and the sensor's supply requirements before connecting it.

Stage 1 — make the data contract work. Generate a short file containing normal values, a missing value and an out-of-range value. Define units and timestamps. Run a parser that retains valid observations and records why it rejected others. Confirm that a missing reading does not become a plausible zero by accident.

Stage 2 — add state and timing. Extend the desktop model using the IDLE, RECORDING, and FAULT table in the embedded chapter. The supplied debounce/heartbeat example demonstrates event handling; it does not already implement this logger. Define what makes a reading stale and whether that condition marks a record invalid, pauses recording, or enters FAULT. Predict and test delayed readings, recovery, and restart before connecting a board. A passing simulation establishes behavior under its inputs, not electrical correctness.

Stage 3 — connect one physical sensor. Check the unpowered wiring against the named pin diagram. Use the chapter's appropriate low-energy supply and measurement procedure. Log a known stable condition and a controlled change. Record warm-up, placement and environmental conditions. If readings move when the cable moves, investigate the interface before adjusting software filters.

Acceptance checks: records retain units and validity; a sensor interruption is distinguishable from a real low reading; the system restarts into a defined state; a second person can reproduce a short measurement from the instructions. Compare a few readings with an appropriate reference where possible. Do not claim calibration from merely matching a single point.

Independent extension: add a local display or a network mirror. Keep the local logger useful when the extension fails. Demonstrate a disconnected network without losing the distinction between fresh and stale data. Keep credentials and personal measurements out of a public repository.

Capstone C: a tabletop positioning system

Result: a simulated axis or two-link arm with explicit coordinates, limits, sensing assumptions and stop behavior, followed only if appropriate by a small physical demonstrator. Study mechanisms, embedded systems, controls and robotics first. The control lab and robotics lab provide runnable starting points.

Materials: the desktop simulation is the first build and needs no motor purchase. A later physical version requires a documented actuator, compatible driver and supply, a suitable support structure, position sensing if the task needs it, and a deliberate means of removing actuator energy. Select these after calculating torque, speed and travel, rather than treating a motor's advertised torque as a complete system specification.

Stage 1 — make the geometry explicit. Draw the reference frame, travel region and obstacles. Predict reachable positions. Run the kinematics examples and check several points by hand. A mathematically reachable point may still collide with a fixture or exceed a joint's physical limit.

Stage 2 — model behavior over time. Use a simple plant model and bounded command. Test target changes, sensor delay, noise and output saturation. Compare error, settling behavior and control effort. A more aggressive controller may reduce one error measure while creating oscillation or larger peak commands. Keep the model's omissions visible.

Stage 3 — plan the physical test before energizing. Identify pinch points, stored energy, travel limits, loose wiring and the consequences of a wrong direction. Design the first test to observe direction and stop behavior at low energy, with the moving assembly secured and no person in its path. Obtain appropriate equipment-specific instruction when needed. An emergency stop is a physical system design issue, not merely a button drawn in an app.

Acceptance checks: the coordinate convention matches observed motion; commands stay within established bounds; stale or invalid sensor input produces the defined response; restarting does not cause an unexpected move; the mechanism's load and travel remain within the tested setup. Increasing payload, speed or reach creates a new validation question.

Independent extension: add an AI perception suggestion only after the baseline works. Evaluate the perception separately and bound what it can command. A classifier's confidence number is not a physical safety guarantee. The pivot joint remains an unpowered mechanical learning project until its own actuation and load requirements have been designed and tested.

Budget time for evidence and rework

A schedule that contains only fabrication and coding is missing integration. In an illustrative ten-hour allocation, reserve two hours for requirements and interface checks, three for the smallest working build, two for integration, two for tests and documentation, and one for rework. These are planning proportions, not a promise that every project fits ten hours. If the uncertainty is large, shorten the first milestone instead of compressing its testing to zero.

Keep a bill of materials with quantity, exact part identifier, supplier, cost basis and acceptable substitutions. A substitution should preserve the relevant specifications, not just the name. A different sensor may need a different voltage; a different bearing may need a different shaft fit. Record consumables and failed iterations too, so your cost model improves.

Choose a few checkpoints at which you decide whether to continue, revise or stop. Useful checkpoints include a working interface coupon, a validated data format and an integrated demonstration. Each should produce something reviewable: measurements, a drawing, a trace or a short video with the conditions stated.

Hand over a reproducible project

A public project needs more than a photograph of the successful moment. Include the purpose, build revision, prerequisites, bill of materials, source files, manufacturing orientation, assembly order, runnable commands, expected outputs, known limitations and actual test record. Distinguish CAD predictions, simulated results and physical measurements. Preserve earlier releases so a reader can identify which version their parts came from.

Before publishing, open the download in a clean folder and follow the instructions as a new reader would. Check relative links, file names and units. Remove credentials, private data and machine-specific paths. State reuse permissions deliberately and check the licenses of third-party code or assets you redistribute. If a project will be sold or used in a regulated setting, investigate the requirements for that product and jurisdiction separately; a successful hobby demonstration is not certification.

For broader project inspiration, Fab Academy combines fabrication, electronics and documented projects through a distributed program. Its public archives can show how others record iterations; enrolled instruction and local lab access are separate from reading those archives. Choose one documented project and study its failed attempts as carefully as its final image.

Final lab: a short design review

Use the project review worksheet. Give a colleague, makerspace mentor or future version of yourself five minutes of evidence: the job, one interface, one prediction, one measured or simulated result and one unresolved risk. Ask them to identify a claim unsupported by the evidence. Revise that claim or design a test.

What counts as finished? The agreed checks have been performed, the result and limitations are recorded, and another person can understand the configuration. Optional improvements can become a new revision.

What if the test fails? Preserve the raw result, identify which assumption it challenges and choose a focused next experiment. Do not silently change the acceptance threshold to turn a failure into a pass.

What should the next project teach? Choose the next constraint your current system exposes: a better fit, a cleaner measurement, a more reliable interface or a more understandable control. That gives further study a purpose and keeps an expanding tool collection connected to things you can explain and build.
